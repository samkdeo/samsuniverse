# Read-Aloud Pacing Checker

Reviews existing manuscript text for how it performs out loud rather than on the page. A sentence that reads fine silently can still stumble when spoken, and this skill catches that gap. It edits for rhythm and flow. It does not restructure plot (route to story-arc-for-young-readers) and does not draft new text from scratch (route to picture-book-writer).

## Step 1: Get the manuscript and age band

Ask for the target age band if it wasn't given. The bar for "trips up a reader" sits in a different place at 0-3, where single words matter, than at 7-9, where a reader can handle more complex sentences.

Manuscripts usually arrive unpaginated, which is normal and not a reason to stop. Propose a pagination, say you've assumed it, and note that the page-turn findings depend on it. Eight lines of board-book verse is four spreads of a couplet each unless the author says otherwise.

## Step 1b: Check the text against the format it claims

Do this before the line-by-line pass, because it is often the real answer and the line notes are downstream of it.

Count the text blocks against the stated page count. A 32-page picture book carries roughly 14 story spreads after front matter. When someone hands you eight blocks of text for a 32-page book, the manuscript is not short of words, it is short of breaks, and the fix is splitting existing text across more turns rather than writing anything new.

Word budgets by band, for judging whether a manuscript is thin or bloated: 100-300 words at 0-3, 400-700 at 4-6, 1,000-2,000 at 7-9. These describe prose. Verse runs well below them, and a rhyming 32-page book at 4-6 is entirely normal at 200-350 words. Never tell a poet their book is thin on a prose word count: padding is the worst thing that can happen to metered verse.

Splitting one dense page across several turns is a pacing fix, not a plot change, so it stays in scope here. Redistributing existing beats is this skill's job; adding, cutting, or reordering beats is story-arc-for-young-readers.

## Step 2: Read every line for these specific issues

Work page by page, or spread by spread, and check each line against the list below. A vague "sounds good" is not a review. Flag specific words and specific lines.

Sentence mechanics:

- Sentences over roughly 12 words for ages 0-6, or over 20 words for 7-9. Long sentences are the most common reason a parent loses their place mid-read. This applies to prose only. In metered verse the line, not the sentence, is the unit of breath, so a single sentence running a whole four-line stanza is fine as long as the line breaks fall on clean grammatical joins. Applied literally to verse this rule condemns good writing, so check it and say you dismissed it rather than reporting it.
- Tongue-twisting consonant clusters, or repeated similar sounds that aren't deliberate wordplay: "the sixth sheep's thick fleece"
- Awkward stress patterns. Say the line naturally and mark where the emphasis actually falls, then check whether the meaning wants it somewhere else. The usual culprit is a function word such as "then", "and", or "was" sitting where the beat lands while the meaning sits on the next word along.
- Lines that only work visually, leaning on punctuation or line breaks the ear can't hear. Picture books are often read from images the child can see, but the words still have to carry their own rhythm.

Repetition:

- If the manuscript uses a repeated refrain, check that it's identical every time it appears. Kids notice and expect exact repetition, and a slightly reworded refrain reads as a mistake rather than variation.
- The comic rule of three is the one real exception: a third beat may break the pattern when the break is the punchline. It does not license a break that is only a grammatical tail. "It didn't fit. It didn't fit. It didn't fit either" changes the words without paying for the change, so make all three identical.
- Watch for accidental echo, which is a different fault: the same distinctive word landing twice within a few lines without being a refrain. It reads as a small stumble and it flattens whatever it lands on.

Narrator intrusion:

- The tacked-on moral, most often in the last line. "And that is the story of the brass key and what it teaches us about curiosity" ends the book twice, and the second ending explains the first. Flag it as a rhythm problem, because that is what it is: the story lands, then keeps talking, and the reader's voice has nowhere to fall.
- Books want to close on a short stressed word the voice can land on. A five-syllable abstract noun in the final position is a dying fall.

Page-turn pacing:

- Does the page-turn land on a natural pause, a question, or a moment of suspense? A page that ends mid-thought with no reason to turn wastes the turn, and a page that gives away the next page's surprise undercuts it.
- Is there a rhythm to how much text sits on each page, or does it swing from one word to a dense paragraph with no story reason?

## Step 2b: If the manuscript rhymes, scan it properly

A large share of picture book submissions rhyme, and "the meter feels off" is usually the reason someone asks for this review at all. Vague confirmation is useless here. Do the arithmetic.

1. **Establish the contract first.** Count syllables and stressed beats per line across the whole manuscript and find its own dominant shape. Do not import an expected one: beat count and syllable count come apart, and a four-beat line runs about 9 syllables in iambic or trochaic metre and 12 or 13 in anapestic. Judging an anapestic manuscript against a 9-syllable expectation flags every line in it. You cannot call a line broken until you know what it broke away from, and the contract is whatever the manuscript is mostly doing.
2. **Judge every line against that shape**, and separate a substitution from a failure. A line that starts on a stress instead of an off-beat is variation and often deliberate. A line a whole beat short is a repair.
3. **Check that rhyming syllables match in stress.** This is the single most common failure in amateur rhyming manuscripts and it is invisible on the page. A one-syllable stressed word paired with a word whose final syllable is unstressed, such as "tree" against "butterfly" or "day" against "holiday", forces the reader to choose between an unnatural wind-up-toy stress on the last syllable and letting the rhyme evaporate. There is no third option, and the reader hits it at speed. A stressed syllable rhymes with a stressed syllable.
4. **Check that the rhyme's landing stays consistent across the whole manuscript.** A rhyme can end on a stressed syllable, which lands the voice on the beat, or on an unstressed trailing syllable, which lets it fall away. Both are fine. Switching between them is what an ear hears as a wobble it cannot name. Note which each stanza uses and flag any drift: a manuscript that lands hard, then soft, then hard has taught the reader a rule twice and broken it twice. Two stanzas is not enough to establish an alternation, so it reads as an error rather than a pattern. The same goes for the unrhymed lines, whose endings often harden or soften across a piece without the author deciding to.

5. **Check for accent-dependent rhymes**, and do not resolve them yourself. The usual traps are again/rain, been/seen, ate/late, either/neither, route/out, and any word whose British and American stress differs. Ask the author to read the couplet aloud in their own accent, since only they can settle it, and say what the answer changes. Spelling in the manuscript is a clue to their accent, never an answer, and a book selling into several markets may have to accept that a rhyme works in one and not another.
6. **Catch rhyme-driven sense errors.** When a rhyme forces a word that means the wrong thing, that belongs here rather than being routed away as a plot problem. The tell is a word that is perfect for the rhyme and slightly wrong for the moment: a character described as setting off when the story has just brought them home, or as waking when the scene has put them to sleep. An adult reading it aloud snags on it every time, usually without being able to say why, and at this age they will read the book many times.

Identify the rhyme scheme before checking anything else, and say whether it is stable. Couplets are not the only option: ABCB is the commonest scheme in picture book verse and rhymes only the second and fourth lines. An author whose group says the book is bumpy usually suspects the rhyme scheme first, so confirming it holds is worth reporting even when it isn't the fault.

The scheme changes the page turns. Splitting a stanza across two spreads is free with couplets, since every turn lands on a resolved rhyme. With ABCB the rhyme resolves on the fourth line, so a mid-stanza split strands the reader on an unresolved rhyme across the turn. That is a genuine reason to turn the page and can be used deliberately, but only where the story also wants a pause. Say where each split falls and whether it helps.

Fixing verse means replacing whole lines, since a metrical repair cannot be done a word at a time. That is the one place in this skill where rewriting rather than flagging is correct, so offer the replacement line with its syllable and beat count shown.

When no replacement preserves the author's content and hits the target shape, which happens often, say so rather than forcing one. Name the constraint, give the two directions available, and let them choose. Handing back a line that scans but loses the phrase they cared about is worse than handing back the problem clearly stated.

Word choice:

- Any word likely to be unfamiliar or hard to decode at that age band. This isn't a reason to cut it automatically, since an occasional stretch word is fine and often good, but flag it so the choice is a real choice.
- Words that are easy to mumble or slur together at speed. Flag these, don't auto-fix them.

## Step 3: Output format

Deliver an annotated pass through the manuscript:

```
Page [N]: "[original line]"
Issue: [specific problem], [why it trips up a read-aloud]
Suggested fix: "[revised line]" (only when you're confident; otherwise describe the problem and let the user rewrite)
```

Close with a short summary: total flags by category, and the single highest-priority fix if the user only has time for one pass.

Say what is working, too, and say why. This is otherwise a purely subtractive checklist, and an author who is told only what to change will often "fix" the best page in the book on the next pass. When a long sentence earns its length, when a list has a cadence worth keeping, or when the refrain discipline is already right, name it and mark it as protected.

## Step 4: Respect the user's voice

Suggest fixes instead of quietly rewriting the whole manuscript. Picture book text is short and every word choice is deliberate, so a wholesale rewrite can strip out intentional style. When a supposed flaw might be a deliberate choice, like a long sentence used once for a breathless, rushing effect, say so rather than filing it as an error.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Vague feedback ("this could flow better") with no specific line and no specific mechanical reason. On a rhyming manuscript this means doing the scansion rather than reporting a feeling about it.
- Flagging stylistically intentional choices as if they were mistakes.
- Checking only sentence-level mechanics and ignoring page-turn pacing. Page turns are half of what makes a picture book work read aloud.
- Auditing the lines while never checking the manuscript against the format it claims to be. Eight text blocks for a 32-page book is the finding, and no amount of line-level polish surfaces it.
