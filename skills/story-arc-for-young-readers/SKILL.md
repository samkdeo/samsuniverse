# Story Arc for Young Readers

Turns a loose idea or a stuck draft into a beat-by-beat outline sized correctly for the target age. The main thing this guards against is adult-story complexity, meaning subplots, ambiguous endings, and multiple POV shifts, smuggled into a kids' story where it doesn't belong. The opposite failure counts too: a story so flat that nothing actually happens.

## Step 1: Get the age band and format

First work out whether they want an outline or an audit. An author who says they have the whole plot already and want to check it holds up is not asking you to generate one, and handing them a fresh outline ignores the work they've done. Ask them to paste their beats, or their chapter endings for a chapter book, and check those against the rules below. When they haven't given you enough to audit, offer a reference spine for their age band and length and invite them to diff their version against it, which is useful immediately and doesn't pretend to be their book.

For everyone else, who is the more common case:

Don't gate the whole outline behind a question round. When you have the age and any idea at all, build a provisional beat sheet, name the assumptions you made, and ask the remaining questions underneath it. A parent who says "that's all I've got really" needs something to react to, not a form to fill in.

So treat the list below as what to establish, by inference where you can, not as a form to send back. Assume the standard 32-page picture book unless something says otherwise, and say you assumed it.

- Age band: 0-3, 4-6, or 7-9. The complexity rules below depend on it.
- Format: picture book (24-32 pages) or early reader chapter book (several short chapters).
- The raw idea: character, setting, and, most importantly, what the character WANTS. If the user gives you only a theme or a moral, push back gently and ask what the character is trying to do. A want is what makes an outline possible, and a theme is not a plot.

Most users arrive with too little rather than too much, usually an event ("she lost a tooth"), an ending they love ("the fairy leaves a note"), or a feeling. Asking "what does she want?" is useless to someone who came to you precisely because they don't know. Generate the want instead: propose two or three candidate wants that lead to the ending they already picked, say which you'd build and why, and let them choose. An event is something that happens to a character. A want is what makes her act. The story needs the second one, and you can usually derive it from the ending in a minute.

Watch what the ending they've chosen does to the child's agency, because this is where the fix belongs rather than at the end of a checklist. Endings people love often hand the resolution to a magical helper: a fairy, a wizard, a talking animal, a kindly adult. Arriving and fixing everything leaves the child a bystander in her own book. Keep the ending and change what causes it, so the gift becomes a reply to something the child did. That reframing is a want-generation move, and it usually improves the ending they already wanted rather than costing them anything.

## Step 2: Apply age-appropriate structural complexity

| Age | Max plot threads | Cast size | Stakes | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3 | 1 | 1-2 characters | Low, concrete (found it or didn't find it) | Always resolves happily, no ambiguity |
| 4-6 | 1, plus at most one light subplot that mirrors the main one | 2-4 characters | Emotional stakes (fear, embarrassment, wanting) are fine; physical danger should be mild | Resolves clearly; the character changes or learns something shown through action |
| 7-9 | 1 main plus 1 real subplot | up to 5-6 characters | Can include real risk, social stakes, mild peril | Can carry a bittersweet note but has to land on hope; loose threads should be intentional, not confusing |

A subplot mirrors the main one when both are the same character wanting the same thing by different means. A fox who wants to join the choir so his mother can hear him, and who brings her a feather every morning, has one want and two expressions of it. Two unrelated problems running side by side is two threads, not a subplot, however small the second one is. Count by wants, not by page space, or ambient pressure gets waved through as atmosphere.

When the user's idea exceeds these limits, say so specifically and offer a concrete alternative. Quietly simplifying it, or quietly leaving it overcomplicated, are both worse.

Be careful which alternative you offer. Bumping up a band buys one extra thread, not unlimited threads, so it only rescues a two-thread idea at 4-6. A genuinely three-thread idea exceeds every band on this table, and the honest answer is that it's two books. Offering "shall we make it 7-9?" to a three-thread brief promises a fix the rules above don't actually allow.

## Step 3: Build the beat sheet

The standard five-beat arc, scaled to the format:

1. Setup: who, where, and what's normal. Keep it short, because kids' attention isn't spent on establishing shots. One spread in a picture book, or half a chapter in a chapter book, is the ceiling.
2. Problem or want: the thing that disrupts normal, or the thing the character wants and doesn't have.
3. Rising attempts: two or three tries that fail or half-work, each one different from the last. Don't repeat the same failed attempt three times. Vary the approach or escalate the stakes.
4. Turning point: the attempt that works, ideally using something set up earlier, like an object, a lesson, or a friend mentioned before, so the resolution feels earned.
5. Resolution and landing beat: show the change, whether that's a new state or a new feeling, and don't state a moral. End on an image or an action, not a summary sentence.

Output the beat sheet as a numbered list, one line per beat, with a page or chapter allocation next to each beat ("Beat 1, pages 1-4").

## Step 3b: When the user wants an ambiguous ending

This comes up often, usually phrased as ambiguity respecting children, which is a real position and not a whim. Give it a real answer rather than asserting the table at them.

Split the ambiguity in two. The emotional question, meaning did the character get what she wanted or find her place, has to resolve. Under about 7, an unresolved belonging question doesn't read as open, it reads as no, and the child goes to bed having heard that the fox never got in. The factual or interpretive question, meaning whether he was really any good, can stay wide open. Describe what happened and let the reader decide what it was.

That gets the writer the ambiguity they actually care about while the child still gets an ending.

The same split governs hard subject matter. An ill parent, a death, a house move, or a divorce can sit in a book for this age, but the child's question about it has to close. The parent does not have to be cured, and a false recovery is worse than none, but there does have to be a visible change of state by the last spread: down in the dark on page 9, sitting up at the window on page 31. Concrete, honest, and not a promise you can't keep.

When the hard subject belongs to another child rather than to the protagonist's own family, which covers poverty, a difficult home, disability, and most playground cruelty, three more things apply.

Do not solve the condition. A parent who gets a job in the last chapter, an anonymous donor, a hamper: these are worse than no ending at all, because a reader living in that situation reads it as proof that fixing only happens to other people. Change the child's standing instead of their circumstances. Something concrete is different about who they are in that room by the last page.

The other child is a character, not a condition. Describing them only through what they lack is both the misery the author is trying to avoid and a way of flattening them. Give them something they are good at that has nothing to do with the hard thing, and establish it before the protagonist finds out.

Let the protagonist get it wrong first. If the discovery scene's emotional content is pity, the book has told the reader what to feel. Anger, fear, or a mistaken assumption, corrected afterwards, reads as honest where instant compassion reads as instructive.

## Step 4: Pacing check

Check the outline against these and flag anything that violates them. Read-aloud runs about 100 words a minute with page-turn pauses, so the minute ceilings convert to word budgets you can actually check an outline against:

| Age | Read-aloud ceiling | Word budget |
|---|---|---|
| 0-3 | under 3 minutes | 100-300 words |
| 4-6 | under 7 minutes | 500-700 words |
| 7-9 picture book | under 10 minutes | 1,000-2,000 words |
| 7-9 chapter book | see below, read-aloud time does not govern | 5,000-12,000 words total |

Read-aloud time governs picture books, where an adult reads at about 100 words a minute to a listening child. It does not govern an early chapter book, which an 8-year-old mostly reads alone. Do not convert chapter length into read-aloud minutes and tell an author their book is too long: a 12,000-word six-chapter book at 7-9 is entirely normal.

No single beat should eat more than 40% of the total page or word budget. If the setup or the rising attempts are ballooning, say so. In a chapter book with fixed equal chapters, convert it: 40% of 12,000 words is about two and a half chapters of rising attempts, and that is the number that bites.

## Chapter books, where the unit is a chapter and not a spread

The five-beat arc holds, but almost everything else on this page is picture-book machinery. Do not hand a chapter-book author spread counts and front matter.

Map the five beats onto their chapter count rather than assuming they match. Six chapters usually goes setup, problem, attempt, attempt, turning point, resolution. Eight gives you a third attempt and room to let the worst moment sit for a chapter. Five means the resolution shares a chapter with the turning point. Say which mapping you used.

Chapter endings are where a book this length lives or dies, and they are the thing most worth checking. Every chapter ends on a hook, a reversal, or a decision, never on the characters going to bed at the end of an uneventful day. Each chapter also wants its own small shape rather than being a slice of continuous narration.

Watch where the inciting discovery lands. It should be done by the end of chapter two. Writers routinely hold it to chapter three or four, which leaves the front of the book with nothing to do.

## Page arithmetic for picture books, so the allocations are real

A 32-page picture book is not 32 pages of story. Pages 1 and 2 go to front matter, leaving pages 3 to 32, which is fifteen spreads. A spread is the two facing pages the reader sees at once, and it's the real unit: one spread is one beat of story and one page-turn.

A workable default split for 32 pages, adjusted to the idea rather than copied blindly:

- Setup: 3 spreads, pages 3-8
- Problem or want: 2 spreads, pages 9-12
- Rising attempts: 6 spreads, pages 13-24, two per attempt
- Turning point: 3 spreads, pages 25-30
- Resolution and landing: 2 spreads, pages 31-32

That puts rising attempts at 40%, right on the ceiling, so if one attempt grows in drafting, shrink another rather than letting the middle sag. The middle is where books for this age lose their listeners.

For 24 pages, cut one spread from setup and one from the attempts. For a board book at 0-3, ignore this table: those run 12 to 24 pages with one line per page, and the five-beat arc compresses to setup, repetition, and a settling ending.

## Step 5: Hand off

Once the outline is approved, note the next step: picture-book-writer for a picture book, or the user's own drafting. For a chapter book, hand back to the user, since picture-book-writer is the wrong tool for 12,000 words of prose. This skill produces the skeleton, not the prose. If the user asks for prose right after the outline, draft it using the approved beats instead of re-litigating structure.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- A resolution that depends on an adult swooping in to fix everything, leaving the child character with no agency. Watch for the disguised version, where a magical helper such as a fairy, a wizard, or a talking animal arrives and hands over the ending. It's fine occasionally, but flag it when it's the only solution on the table, and look for a way to make the gift a reply to something the child did.
- Vague beats ("things get complicated") in place of a concrete, stageable action.
- Accepting the user's brief as given when it breaks the age band, then producing a polished outline that looks right and reads wrong to the child it was written for.
