# Gift Idea Tracker

Two jobs. **Capturing** a hint at the moment it is dropped, in a form that will still be useful in eight months, and **choosing** from the captured record when an occasion arrives. Most of the value is in the first job, done cheaply enough that it actually gets done, and most of the failure is in capturing an interpretation instead of the thing that was said.

This does not browse for products or generate a gift guide from demographics. A list of plausible presents for "a 60-year-old man who likes gardening" is worth nothing, and producing one when the record is empty hides the real answer, which is that nobody has been paying attention and the fix starts now.

## The most important thing to say first

You will not remember this. Neither will the user, unless it lands somewhere they will actually look.

Say where the record lives before writing anything into it, and be blunt that a note kept only inside a chat is lost. It needs to be a file, a notes app, a shared list with a partner, or a calendar entry — somewhere they already go. Ask which, or if they have said, use it. If the user wants a file, produce it and hand it over as a file rather than as text in a reply.

This is the single reason gift trackers fail, and no amount of good capture survives it.

## When a year of hints arrives at once

The common case is not a hint dropped live. It is someone six weeks from an occasion, emptying out everything they can half-remember from the past year, none of it verbatim. The instruction to quote exactly cannot be followed on arrival, and refusing to log a paraphrase is worse than logging one badly.

So log what they have, and mark the recall quality as part of the entry rather than pretending. Three grades are enough: their words, your paraphrase, or second-hand. Then say what each downgrade costs, because it is specific rather than general. A paraphrase loses the size of the want and the excuse attached to it, which are exactly the two things that decide whether something is a good gift, and "wanted to get back into cycling" and "I keep meaning to get my bike out of the garage" are different presents.

Ask the user to overwrite any paraphrase with the real words if they surface later, and put that instruction in the file rather than only in the reply.

Date a backfilled hint with what is actually known — a season, a month, "sometime last year" — rather than today's date, which would make a stale hint look fresh. If the date is genuinely unknown, write unknown.

## Capturing a hint

Keep this to a few seconds of work. A capture format with eight fields does not get used twice.

Record four things:

- **The person.**
- **What they said, in their words.** Quote it. This matters more than anything else here and it is the part people paraphrase away. "I've been meaning to replace that saucepan for about six years" is a usable hint; "kitchen stuff" is not, and it is what the paraphrase turns into by March. The exact words carry the size of the want, whether they have already looked, and often the reason they have not bought it.
- **The date.** Not optional. A hint's value decays and the rate depends on the hint, so the date is what makes that judgeable later.
- **Where it came from**, in a few words. Overheard, said to you directly, seen on their wishlist, told to you by their partner. This decides whether the gift will land as thoughtful or as slightly unnerving, and it decides whether you can ask a follow-up question without giving anything away.

Then two judgements, which are what make the record worth keeping.

The first is **what is stopping them**, which is usually stated in the hint itself and is the most actionable thing in it, because it says what the gift actually has to supply. Money is only one of four, and assuming it is the answer produces the dull version of every gift.

- **Money.** They want it and cannot justify the spend. The gift is the money.
- **Effort or research.** They want it, could afford it, and cannot face working out which one. The gift is the decision, and it lands well above its price because it removes a chore they have been avoiding. Under-recognised, and common in anything with too many options.
- **Knowledge or access.** They do not know where to get it, who does it, or that it exists. The gift is the introduction.
- **Permission.** They think it is silly, indulgent, or not for people like them. The gift is someone else deciding it is fine.

Write the blocker down in the hint's own words where possible, because "I can't be bothered to look into it" and "I could never justify it" point at completely different presents at the same price.

The second is the category. These are not exclusive, and a real hint usually touches two. Do not force one; write the one that dominates first and note the other. Where two genuinely compete, the blocker decides, since it is what the gift has to overcome:

- **Would not buy for themselves.** Too expensive to justify, too frivolous, feels indulgent, or they think they do not deserve it. This is where the best gifts live, and the tell is usually in the phrasing — a want stated and then immediately excused. "I'd love one but honestly, for the two times a year I'd use it." Flag these hard, because they are the ones the user will otherwise pass over as too obvious.
- **Wants and will buy shortly.** They intend to get it themselves. This is a bad gift target unless the occasion is close, and it needs the freshness check below.
- **A running joke or an enthusiasm**, not a specific object. Useful context, weak as an instruction. It is where a merely fine gift comes from, and where most well-intentioned bad gifts come from too: an interest is not a shopping list, and someone with four hundred records does not need a fifth chosen by someone who does not know the four hundred.
- **A stated dislike or an anti-hint.** Log these. "Please, no more scented anything" is as valuable as a want and is remembered far less often.
- **A need they have not solved.** Sometimes the right gift; sometimes it reads as a comment on how they live. Note it and decide at choosing time, not now.

Do not editorialise the hint into a product. "She said she wants a nicer chopping board" should not become "bamboo end-grain board, £40" in the record, because in nine months the guess is indistinguishable from the quote and the guess is what gets bought. Keep the quote, put any candidate product on a separate line, marked as yours.

One line per hint. Append, never reorganise.

The floor is person, words, and date. Those three make an entry usable; the blocker and the category can be added later, or left blank when the hint does not say. Do not stall a capture to complete a field, and do not guess a blocker to fill the slot, because an invented blocker reads a year later exactly like an observed one.

## Handling other people's information decently

This is a record about people who did not ask to be recorded, so keep it proportionate. Capture wants and dislikes. Do not build a file of someone's insecurities, finances, health, or relationship complaints because it might inform a purchase, and do not log something told to you in confidence in a way that would embarrass the teller if the file were read aloud. The test is simple: would the person be pleased or unsettled to see their own entry? A tracker that fails that test is not a nicer gift, it is a dossier, and someone will eventually find it.

Where a hint came from a third party, note that. It changes what can be said when the gift is opened.

## Buying for a group on a fixed budget

A team at Christmas, a class, a club, a group of cousins with a set amount each. This changes several things and the file has to be set up for it from the start.

**Where it lives changes.** When the subjects are people who work for or report to the user, the file must not sit on a shared drive, in a team space, or anywhere a colleague could open it. What reads as thoughtful in a personal notebook reads as a manager keeping files on staff when it is found on a company system. Say this before the format.

**The proportionality test gets stricter, not looser.** A power gradient means the subject cannot easily object to being recorded. Wants and dislikes only. Nothing about anyone's money, health, home life, or how they seem to be coping, however useful it would be for choosing. The gift is not worth the file.

**Money is rarely the blocker at a per-head budget**, and it cannot be. A modest fixed sum does not solve anyone's money problem, and a gift that tries reads as a comment on their salary. So the other three blockers do all the work here, and effort and knowledge do most of it.

**Aim for equal specificity rather than equal spend.** Spend is already equal; that is what a per-head budget means. The gap that shows is between the two people the user knows well and the ones they barely know, and it shows most on the ones who get the generic thing. Two uncannily well-observed gifts and ten candles is a worse outcome than twelve gifts that are all mildly specific. Where the file is thin on someone, aim lower and more precise rather than higher and vaguer.

**Backfill the whole roster in one pass**, one line per person, including the people the user has nothing for. The blank lines are the point of the exercise: they say exactly who to pay attention to over the coming months, which is the thing that makes next year different from this year.

## Choosing, when the occasion arrives

Get the occasion, the date, the budget, and the relationship. Then work the record.

1. **Freshness check first.** Go through the candidates and ask which have probably resolved themselves. The decay rate depends on the type of hint, not on a fixed number of months. A stated intention to buy something soon goes stale quickly. A long-standing gripe about a thing they have put up with for years is not going stale at all, and is often the best entry in the file precisely because it has survived. Seasonal and situational hints expire with the situation — an item for a trip, a house they have since left, a hobby they have dropped. Say which candidates need checking and how to check without spoiling it, which is usually a question to their partner or a sibling rather than to them.
2. **Filter by the occasion, not just the budget.** The same object is a good birthday present and a strange Valentine's one. Note what the occasion's conventions are in this particular relationship, and where the user has a history to be consistent with or to deliberately break.
3. **Check the fit risk before committing to an object.** Some things cannot be chosen for someone else without a good chance of being wrong: anything sized, fitted, prescribed, or matched to a body, a room, or an existing set they own. A mattress, a bike, glasses, anything medical, anything that has to match a machine they already have. Getting it wrong turns the gift into a drawer ornament plus an awkward conversation about returning it.

   The move is to fund the thing rather than to buy it: pay for the fitting, the assessment, or the appointment, and cover the purchase up to a stated amount, leaving the choice with them. That removes the blocker the hint named while leaving the decision where it belongs. Say so explicitly rather than presenting a specific model.

4. **Rank, and give a first choice.** Do not hand back five options of equal weight. The user asked because deciding is the hard part. Give one recommendation, one backup at a different price point, and one that is cheap and could be added to either.
5. **Say what evidence each rests on**, with the date and the quote. This is what turns a suggestion into something the user can trust, and it lets them overrule you on grounds you do not have.
6. **Name the gap where there is one.** If everything in the file is thin, say so plainly and give two or three specific things to listen for over the next few weeks, tied to that person. That is more useful than a confident recommendation built on nothing.

Where the record is empty and the occasion is close, do not generate a demographic list. Ask the small number of questions that actually discriminate: what did they get last year and how did it land, what have they bought themselves recently, what do they complain about, what would they never buy for themselves. Four answers to those beats any amount of browsing.

## After the occasion

Close the loop, briefly, because this is what makes the record better over time rather than merely longer.

- Mark what was given, and the date. This prevents the most common failure in a long-running tracker, which is buying a near-duplicate of something given two years ago.
- Note how it landed, honestly. A one-word note is enough. A gift that was received politely and never used is information, and it is only available now.
- Remove the entry that was used. Leave the ones that were not, and leave the anti-hints permanently.

## Output format

For capture, one line per hint, appended, with an optional second indented line for anything that is yours rather than theirs:

```
[date] [person] — "[words]" [recall: theirs / paraphrase / second-hand] — [source] — [blocker] — [category]
    > candidate (mine, not hers): [product guess, and the question that would settle it]
```

The indent is doing real work. It is what keeps a guess from being read as a quote in eight months, and it is why the candidate does not go on the hint line.

For choosing, a short recommendation with the evidence attached:

```
For [person], [occasion], [budget]
  First choice: [thing] — from "[quote]" ([date])
  Backup: [thing] — [why]
  Add-on: [thing]
  Stale, check before buying: [entries]
  Nothing on file about: [gap]
```

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Offering to remember something without saying that the record has to live somewhere the user will look. Everything else here is downstream of that.
- Paraphrasing the hint into a category, so the specific and usable thing becomes "kitchen stuff" within a month.
- Recording your product guess in the same line as their words, so the guess is what gets bought a year later.
- Ignoring the date, then recommending something they bought themselves in March.
- A capture format long enough that the second hint never gets logged.
- Producing a demographic gift guide when the file is empty, which looks like an answer and hides the actual finding.
- Handing back five equally weighted options to someone who came because they could not decide.
- Losing the anti-hints, which are the cheapest way to avoid a bad gift and are almost never written down.
- Refusing to log a paraphrase because it is not verbatim, or logging one as though it were.
- Dating a backfilled hint with today's date, so a year-old want looks fresh at the freshness check.
- Assuming the blocker is money, which produces the expensive version of a gift whose real obstacle was that nobody wanted to spend an evening comparing models.
- Buying a specific sized, fitted, or prescribed object rather than funding it, so the gift is a return trip.
- Logging things about a person that they would be unsettled to read about themselves, or keeping a file on people who report to you somewhere the company can open it.
- Spending the same on everyone in a group and calling that fair, while the people you know least get the least specific gift.
