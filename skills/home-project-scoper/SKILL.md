# Home Project Scoper

Turns "I want to redo the bathroom" into a list of decisions, a sequence, and a set of things that will otherwise be discovered halfway through. The output is a plan someone can act on and take to a tradesperson, not a quote and not an instruction manual.

## What this does not do

State this once, briefly, and then be useful.

- **It does not price work.** Costs vary by region, by year, by property, and by who is available, and a confident number here is worse than none because it becomes the budget. What it does instead is say which items are the large ones, which are volatile, and what makes a quote go up.
- **It does not rule on permissions.** Whether something needs planning permission, building control, a party wall agreement, listed building consent, or a landlord's approval depends on the property and the jurisdiction and changes. Name which of these might apply as things to check, and say who checks them.
- **It does not advise on anything that can hurt someone or the building.** Structural alterations, electrics, gas, and drainage go to a qualified person, and in many places must legally be done or certified by one. Say what has to be brought in and when it has to be booked, which is scheduling information and genuinely useful, rather than how to do it.

Where the user's real question is one of these, say so plainly and answer the part that is scoping.

## Step 1: Find out what problem is actually being solved

Ask before scoping, because the answer frequently changes the project.

- **What is wrong with it now?** A specific complaint — no storage, cold, dark, the layout does not work, it looks dated — leads to a different and usually much smaller project than "redo it".
- **How long are they staying?** A house being lived in for twenty years and one being sold in two justify entirely different work.
- **Is any of it forced?** A leak, a failure, damp, or something that has to be done anyway is a different project from a discretionary one, and it changes the order.
- **What is the actual deadline**, and is it real? A baby, a visit, a sale, a tenancy. A fixed date changes what is possible more than the budget does.
- **Are they living in it during the work?** This is the question people answer last and it has the biggest effect on sequencing, duration, and how bad the experience is.

Where the complaint is specific and small, say that the small project is the answer. A great deal of renovation is scope that grew because nobody asked what was wrong.

## Step 2: Draw the boundary, and then look just outside it

Write down what is in and what is out, explicitly, because the gap between them is where the budget goes.

Then check the edges, since almost every project touches something adjacent that nobody counted:

- Where the new work meets the old: flooring that now runs into a different flooring, a wall that gets opened, a threshold, a ceiling line that no longer matches.
- The room that gets worse by comparison, which is the commonest unplanned expansion of all.
- Anything that has to come out to allow access, and whether it survives being removed.
- Services that have to move to allow the new layout, which is usually the largest hidden item.
- Making good afterwards: the plastering, the decorating, and the bits that are always underestimated because they are not the interesting part.

Name the likely growth explicitly, as a decision rather than as a surprise: this is where the project will want to expand, and here is where you might choose to stop.

## Step 3: Separate the decisions from the work

The single most useful thing this skill does. Most delays are not the work being slow, they are a decision that had not been made and was needed on the day.

List the decisions in the order they are needed, which is not the order they seem urgent:

- **Decisions that fix the layout**, needed first, because everything downstream depends on where things go.
- **Decisions that determine what has to be built into the structure**, needed before the walls close: anything recessed, anything that needs a cable, a pipe, or a drain, anything that needs support behind a surface. These are the expensive ones to change later and the ones people leave until they are choosing colours.
- **Decisions with long lead times**, which have to be made early regardless of when they are installed. Say plainly that anything ordered rather than bought off a shelf may take much longer than expected, and that the delivery date is what to plan against rather than the order date.
- **Decisions that can genuinely wait**, which is most of the visible finishes.

The cheap thing that is needed early and blocks everything is worth calling out specifically, because people spend their decision-making energy on the expensive things.

## Step 4: Sequence it

The order is largely fixed by physics, and getting it wrong costs the most.

The general shape, adapted to the project:

1. Survey, and find out what is actually there. Old buildings differ from expectations and this is where the discoveries happen.
2. Anything requiring permission or approval, started early because it takes time rather than effort.
3. Strip out and demolition.
4. Structural work.
5. First fix: everything that goes inside walls, floors, and ceilings — services, framing, anything buried.
6. Close up: plastering and the wet trades, which then need time to dry.
7. Second fix: everything that attaches to a finished surface.
8. Decoration.
9. Flooring, usually late.
10. Snagging, which is a real stage and should be in the plan.

Three sequencing rules that cause the most trouble when broken:

- **Anything that gets buried has to be decided and installed before it is covered.** Obvious in the abstract and violated constantly.
- **Drying and curing time is not work time and cannot be compressed.** It sits in the schedule doing nothing and cannot be shortened by paying someone more, and in cold or damp conditions it takes longer.
- **Work from the top down and from the dirty to the clean.** Anything that generates dust comes before anything that gets ruined by dust.

Where several trades are involved, note who has to come back a second time, because return visits are scheduled around someone else's calendar and are a common source of dead weeks.

## Step 5: The things people forget

Not exhaustive, but these recur:

- **Where does everything go?** The contents of the room, and where they live for the duration.
- **Waste.** Renovation produces a startling volume of it, and removal has to be arranged, and sometimes there is nowhere to put a skip.
- **Access.** Parking, getting materials in, getting large old items out, stairs, a narrow door, whether a lorry can get down the road.
- **Living without the room.** Especially a kitchen or a bathroom, which is not a detail but the main experience of the project.
- **Utilities being off**, and for how long, and whether that is compatible with the household.
- **Neighbours**, noise, shared walls, and anything that requires their agreement or their goodwill.
- **Pets and small children**, and what dust, open doors, and tools mean for them.
- **Deliveries arriving before there is anywhere to put them**, which is the standard failure with anything large ordered early.
- **The last ten per cent**, which takes far longer than it should and is where projects stall for months.
- **Cleaning afterwards**, which is a real job and not the same as the builder's tidy.

## Step 6: Say where the uncertainty is

An honest plan says which parts might change and why, rather than presenting a schedule with false precision.

- **Discovery risk** is highest wherever something is opened up for the first time, and is higher in older buildings.
- **Anything with an unknown behind it** — a wall, a floor, a boxed-in section — carries a contingency in time as well as money.
- **A dependency on one person** is the commonest scheduling risk, since a single trade being ill or delayed pushes everything behind them.
- **Long-lead items** determine the end date more often than the work does.

Say what the user should decide now about contingency: what they will do if a discovery adds cost, and what comes out of the plan first if it does. Deciding that in advance is what stops a mid-project scramble.

## Step 7: Deliver

Give back:

1. The project as scoped, with what is deliberately out
2. The decisions, in the order they are needed, with the ones that block everything marked
3. The sequence, with the drying and waiting time visible as its own item
4. The specialists to bring in, and when each needs to be booked rather than when they arrive
5. The permissions and approvals to check, and who checks them
6. The forgotten things that apply here
7. The uncertainty, and the contingency decision
8. What to do first, this week

Keep the first item honest. If the project as described is much larger than the problem being solved, say so at the top.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Scoping the project asked for without checking what problem it solves, when the smaller project would have solved it.
- Giving a cost, which becomes the budget and is wrong.
- Ruling on whether something needs permission.
- Listing the work and not the decisions, when it is the decisions that cause the delays.
- Leaving buried items until the finishes stage, by which point they cost ten times as much.
- A schedule with no drying time in it.
- Ignoring the adjacent room, the threshold, and the making good.
- Forgetting that the household has to live through this.
- Presenting a sequence as certain when the first day of opening things up may change it.
- Ordering long-lead items too late, or too early with nowhere to store them.
