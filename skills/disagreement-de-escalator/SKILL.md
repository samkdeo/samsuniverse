Use this skill when someone shares an argument they are in and asks what to say, or says "how do I reply to this", "this email made me furious", "am I being unreasonable", "I don't want to back down but", "help me not make this worse", "read this before I send it", or pastes a thread and a draft. Use it too when they have written something in anger and want it checked before sending. It does not tell the user they are wrong on the merits, and it is not the right tool for harassment, abuse, or a situation that needs documenting rather than answering.

# Disagreement De-escalator

Produces one message: cooler than the draft, no weaker on substance, and aimed at a stated outcome rather than at winning the exchange. The central move is the separation the user cannot make while angry — conceding tone, impact, and whatever the other person has legitimately right, while holding the actual position completely.

It does not adjudicate the disagreement. The user may be wrong, and if something in the thread suggests they are, say so once, briefly, as information they can use, and then help them write the message they asked for. Refusing to help until they concede is not the job.

## Most of these are one judgement and one rewrite

The steps below are the full apparatus and most requests do not need all of it. Someone who pastes a draft and asks whether to send it usually needs three things: whether to send, what to cut, and the rewrite. Do those and stop.

Run the whole procedure when the disagreement is ongoing rather than a single exchange, when the outcome is genuinely unclear, or when one of the checks below fires. Do not walk a user through eight steps to fix two sentences.

Three cases carry most of the value and are worth going to directly when they apply: a personal rather than professional argument, an accusation that is partly true, and a thread that strangers can read. They are steps 5b, 5c, and 5d.

## First, check this is the right tool

Some situations look like a heated disagreement and are not, and de-escalation makes several of them worse. Read the thread for these before drafting:

- **Harassment, abuse, threats, or a pattern of targeting.** A measured reply to abuse is not de-escalation, it is engagement, and it often produces more. The tools here are documentation, reporting, blocking, and getting a third party involved.
- **A situation that needs a record more than a reply.** Where there is a real prospect of a formal process — a dispute with an employer, a contract that may end badly, anything with a legal or HR shadow — what the user writes is evidence first and communication second. Say so, and write accordingly: factual, dated, unemotional, and free of the concessions that would be perfectly wise in a normal disagreement and unwise in a record.
- **Bad faith.** Where the other party is not trying to resolve anything, conceding a point does not lower the temperature, it gets pocketed. The tell is a pattern across the thread rather than one bad message: moving goalposts, no engagement with anything conceded, escalating regardless of content.
- **A power imbalance the reply cannot fix**, such as a manager, a landlord, or a client with the ability to act unilaterally. The advice is not different in kind but the risk is asymmetric, and the user should decide with that stated rather than discover it.

Where one of these applies, say which and why, and offer what is actually useful. Then, if the user still wants the reply drafted, draft it.

## Second, ask whether to reply at all, and where

Two decisions come before the wording and matter more than it.

**Not every message needs an answer.** Some threads end when someone stops feeding them. If the user's real goal is achievable without a reply, say so. This is genuinely often the best available move and it is almost never the one someone is considering when they arrive angry.

**Written channels are bad at this.** Text strips tone, invites rereading, produces a record, and lets both parties compose their best shots at leisure. Where the relationship matters and the disagreement is real, the highest-value advice is frequently to move it: a call, a walk, ten minutes in person. If that is available, say it plainly, and then draft the short message that proposes it rather than the long one that argues the point.

Moving channels is not always right. Where the substance is factual and needs to be pinned down, where a record is wanted, where the other party is unreliable about what was agreed, or where the user is more articulate in writing than in a confrontation, writing is correct. Say which case this is rather than defaulting to either.

**And on timing:** almost everyone should wait longer than they want to. Anger produces sentences that feel proportionate for a few hours and read as unhinged for years, and nothing on a thread like this is improved by arriving eleven minutes after the provocation. But do not apply this blindly, because some things genuinely need a fast answer: a factual error spreading to other people, a decision about to be made on a false premise, a customer or client waiting. Where speed matters, the right move is a short holding reply now and the substantive one later, not silence.

## Step 1: Separate what happened from what it meant

Get the thread if it has not been given, and read it for three separate things, because the user has them fused.

- **What was actually said**, quoted, in the other person's words.
- **What the user believes it meant.** Almost always more hostile than the text supports. Written messages are read at the emotional temperature of the reader, terseness reads as contempt, and a message written in ninety seconds between meetings reads as a considered insult.
- **What the user is actually upset about**, which is frequently not the thing in the thread. A disproportionate reaction to a small message usually means the small message was the latest instance of something older, and that older thing is what needs saying, if anything does.

Say this back plainly and without diagnosing the user. The goal is not to talk them out of being angry, which does not work and is patronising, but to separate the part that is on the page from the part that is interpretation. Only the first can be replied to. Replying to the interpretation, as though it were the text, is what turns a disagreement into a fight, because the other person receives an accusation about something they did not say.

Where the interpretation might be right, do not talk them out of it either. Say that the text supports either reading and that the reply which assumes the better one costs nothing if wrong and helps enormously if right.

## Step 2: Get the outcome, and check it is achievable

Ask what the user wants to be true after this exchange. Push past the first answer, which is usually a version of being agreed with.

Sort what they say into:

- **An outcome**, such as a decision changed, work unblocked, a boundary respected, a relationship intact, an apology.
- **A feeling**, such as being right, being seen to be right, or the other person understanding how unreasonable they were.

Feelings are legitimate but they are not achievable by a message, and a reply aimed at one produces a longer thread. The specific thing to name is that an admission of fault is not a realistic outcome of a message written in the middle of a disagreement, and building a reply around extracting one guarantees another round.

Then check the outcome is achievable through this person, and this channel. Where the actual decision sits elsewhere, the reply is a different message to a different recipient.

Write the outcome at the top of the draft as a note to the user, so every line can be checked against it. Anything in the message that does not serve it is there for the user's satisfaction and will cost them.

## Step 3: Concede the true thing, specifically

This is the move that lowers temperature, and it is the one people skip because it feels like losing.

Find the part of the other person's position that is legitimately right, and say it plainly. There is almost always something: a fact they have correct, a consequence the user did not consider, a way the user's own message landed badly, a delay that was real, a frustration that is reasonable even if the conclusion is not.

Two rules make this work.

**Be specific.** A vague acknowledgement is worse than none. "I hear you" and "I understand your frustration" are recognisable as the things people say instead of engaging, and they reliably increase anger, because they perform listening without demonstrating it. Name the actual thing: the specific point that is right, the specific way the message landed, the specific mistake.

**Do not attach a but.** A concession followed immediately by "but" is heard as a preamble and cancels itself. Let it stand as its own sentence, or its own paragraph, and start the next thought with a full stop between them. "And" carries the same weight without the reversal.

Concede tone and impact freely, since these cost nothing and are usually true: the user can accept that a message landed badly without accepting it was wrong. Do not concede substance the user believes is right, and be careful to distinguish the two out loud, because a user who is anxious will over-concede and regret it, and a user who is angry will refuse to concede anything at all. Both need to be told which is which.

Never apologise for something the user does not think they did. An insincere apology is detectable, it does not resolve anything, and it becomes a fact in the record.

## Step 4: Hold the position, plainly

The point of the skill is not to be nicer at the cost of the argument. State the substantive position clearly, once, without hedging.

- **Say it in plain declarative sentences.** Hedging reads as weakness and invites another round; aggression invites the same. The register that works is neutral and specific, which most people have to be talked into because it feels insufficiently forceful.
- **Do not relitigate the whole thread.** Pick the one or two things that actually matter to the outcome and drop the rest, including points the user has won. Winning a peripheral point costs more than it gains, and a reply that answers every line is read as an attack whatever its tone.
- **Do not defend against an accusation that was not made.** Anticipatory defence introduces a charge into the record and reads as guilt.
- **Where the disagreement is factual, put the facts in and leave them alone.** Dates, what was agreed, what was sent. Facts stated neutrally are far more powerful than facts stated triumphantly, and adding "as you can see" undoes the work.

## Step 5: Strip the escalators

These are what actually make a message read as hostile, and they survive every attempt to "make it more polite" because they are structural rather than tonal.

Cut every one:

- **Sarcasm and rhetorical questions.** "Did you actually read what I sent?" is an accusation shaped like a question. There is no such thing as a de-escalating rhetorical question.
- **Score-keeping.** "As I said", "as previously mentioned", "per my last email", "to reiterate", "just to be clear", "for the third time". Every one of these says the other person is not paying attention, and that is the message received regardless of the surrounding words.
- **The pointed thank you** and any pleasantry doing sarcastic work. These are the most transparent devices in written argument and everyone recognises them.
- **Absolutes and character claims.** "Always", "never", "you clearly", "you obviously", "typical". A claim about a pattern invites a defence of the whole pattern; a claim about one instance can be discussed.
- **Diagnosing the other person's motives or state.** "You're being defensive", "you clearly don't care", "I'm not sure why this is so difficult". Nobody has ever conceded a point about their own motives.
- **Emphasis carrying anger.** Capitals, bold on the accusatory word, exclamation marks, ellipses that imply something unsaid.
- **Quoting the thread back line by line.** Point-by-point rebuttal is an interrogation format. It reads as prosecution however carefully each point is worded.
- **Adding people.** Copying in a manager or a group mid-argument is understood as escalation, whatever the stated reason. If someone genuinely needs to be involved, say so openly first.
- **The exit lines.** "I don't want to argue about this", "let's agree to disagree", "I'll leave it there", "do what you like". Each of these claims to end the conversation while landing a final hit, and each reliably produces another message.

Then read the draft as though it were written by someone who dislikes the user. Whatever can be read as an attack will be, because the recipient is also angry and reading uncharitably. Rewrite those lines rather than defending them.

## Step 5b: When the disagreement is personal rather than professional

Family, partners, and old friends escalate through different machinery, and the work-shaped rules above miss most of it. Everything already said still applies; these are the additions.

**The competing ledger is the main trap.** In a personal argument the accusation is very often that the other person does less, cares less, or is not really carrying it. The instinctive reply is a tally: what the user actually does, how often, how recently. It is usually accurate, it is the part they most want to send, and it is the single most reliable way to make things worse, because a tally can always be met with a bigger tally and the other person generally has one. Cut it from the message and say why. The same content works spoken, in the form of an offer rather than a rebuttal — asking what would actually help lands where listing what has already been done does not.

**Do not compete on who cares more.** Once an exchange is about the relative depth of two people's love or concern, nothing can be resolved and the damage outlasts the dispute by years.

**Expect the real subject to be older than the thread.** Personal arguments are frequently the current instance of a long-running division, and a reply that treats it as a fresh disagreement will feel to the other person like being deliberately misunderstood. This does not mean raising the older thing in a message. It means recognising that a proportionate-looking reply may not land, and that the older thing is a conversation rather than a text.

**The audience is invisible but present.** There is usually no cc, and the message will still be repeated to other family members, remembered, and quoted back years later. Write the sentence that survives being read aloud at a much later date.

**Both people are usually frightened rather than hostile**, particularly where the argument is about someone's health, money, or a decision nobody wants to make. Saying that to the user is often more useful than any wording, because it changes what they think they are replying to.

## Step 5c: When the accusation is partly true

The hardest and most common case, and the one where users most want help they will not like.

The temptation is to argue the false half, because it is arguable. Doing that reads as a denial of the whole thing, and it hands the other person the true half as proof.

Take the true part first, plainly, before anything else, and take it without conditions. Then hold the position on the rest. This is exactly the concede-tone-hold-substance move, applied to a case where what is being conceded is a fact rather than a tone, and it is more effective here than anywhere else: an accusation that has been partly granted loses almost all its force, while an accusation that has been wholly denied gains it.

Say plainly to the user which half is true, once, and without moralising. That is information they need in order to write anything that works, and delivering it gently but clearly is more use than agreeing with them.

**Where the disagreement rests on a judgement neither party can settle by arguing** — whether something is bad enough, safe enough, ready enough — say so, and look for the thing that would convert it into a question with an answer. An assessment, a professional opinion, a trial period, a measurement, a third party with standing. This is frequently the most valuable move available and it is not a wording change at all: it replaces an argument nobody can win with a process both people can accept, and it gives the other person something concrete instead of the sense that they are carrying the thing alone.

## Step 5d: When the thread is public

A review and its reply, a comment thread, a post someone has quoted, a dispute playing out where strangers can read it. The whole file above assumes two people and a private channel, and several of its instructions invert here.

**The audience is not the other party.** It is people reading later, who mostly do not know either party and are forming an impression in seconds. That changes what a reply is for: not to convince the counterparty, who will not be convinced, but to be the thing a stranger reads. Work out what the thread currently looks like to that reader before writing anything, because it is frequently already favourable, and a further reply can only flatten a clear asymmetry into a story about two people arguing, in which readers take nobody's side.

**Silence is a much stronger move here than in private.** Where the user's account is already on the page and the reply against it is intemperate, the reply is doing the user's work for them. Say so, and say plainly that not answering is a decision rather than a retreat.

**Do not concede into a public record**, and especially not where anything legal-sounding has been said. The generous softening that is wise in a private disagreement becomes a quotable admission. This is the one place where the concede-generously instruction above is wrong, and it should be stated as an exception rather than quietly dropped.

**Preserve the record now.** Screenshot the exchange with dates visible before anything is edited or deleted, and get the underlying evidence out of the platform. This costs an hour today and is unavailable later.

**Do not edit or soften the original under pressure.** An edit made after a threatening reply is read as a retraction, by the counterparty and by everyone else.

**Do not reply to commenters**, including supportive ones, which extends the thread and attaches the user to whatever those people go on to say.

Where a reply is genuinely necessary, it is short, factual, adjective-free, answers none of the accusations, and offers a private channel. Answering an accusation is what keeps it on the page, and an unfalsifiable characterisation cannot be disproved but can be left visibly unanswered.

## Step 6: Check the audience

Who else can see this changes what it should say.

A message on a thread with other people on it is partly a performance, and both parties know it, which is why group threads escalate faster than private ones — conceding in front of an audience costs more, so people do it less. Where that dynamic is driving the heat, the single most effective move is to take it out of the group: a short neutral note in the thread and the substance in private.

Where the message will be read later by someone who was not there — a manager, a court, a board, a future employer — write for that reader too. The good news is that the message which reads well to a third party is almost always the same one that de-escalates, since both reward calm, specific, factual writing and punish point-scoring.

## Step 7: End it in a way that can be answered

Give the recipient something easy to do. A message that ends with a specific, small, answerable proposal converts an argument into a decision. A message that ends with a restatement of the position leaves nothing to do except disagree again.

Say plainly whether the user should have the last word. Usually they should not. The urge to reply to a reply, particularly to something that is nearly agreement but has one irritating line in it, is what keeps threads alive, and it is worth naming as the specific trap it is: the final small correction costs the whole de-escalation.

## Step 8: Deliver

Hand back:

1. The draft, ready to send.
2. The outcome it is written toward, in one line.
3. What was conceded and what was deliberately held, named separately, so the user can see they have not given ground on substance.
4. What was cut and why, briefly, especially anything satisfying that had to go.
5. Anything that should be said in person or by phone instead.
6. What to do if the reply is hostile, decided now rather than in the next surge of anger.

Tell the user to sit on it before sending. Reading it once more, later, is the cheapest quality control available.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Producing a politer version of the same attack, with the escalators intact under softer vocabulary.
- Conceding substance while trying to concede tone, so the user wakes up having agreed to something they do not believe.
- "I hear you" and "I understand your frustration", which are recognised as the things people say instead of listening.
- A concession with "but" welded to it, which cancels the concession and reads as a run-up.
- Replying to what the user thinks the message meant rather than to what it said, so the other person receives an accusation about something they did not write.
- Answering every point in the thread, which is a prosecution regardless of tone.
- Aiming at an admission of fault, which is not something a message obtains.
- Drafting a careful written reply where a five-minute call would end it.
- Sending inside the hour, when nothing on the thread required it.
- Treating abuse or a formal dispute as a disagreement to be smoothed over.
- Answering an accusation that the user does less with a list of what they do, which invites a longer list back.
- Arguing the false half of a half-true accusation, which reads as denying all of it.
- Treating a personal argument with work-email rules, when it is running on history rather than on the thread.
- Perfecting the wording of a dispute that could be converted into a question with an actual answer.
- Applying private-thread instincts to a public one, so the user concedes generously into a permanent record in front of strangers.
- Replying at all when the thread as it stands already favours the user, and only a reply can spoil it.
- Helping the user get the last word, which is the most common way a de-escalated thread reignites.
