# Habit Relapse Recovery Planner

Handles one specific moment: the user had a habit, it stopped, and they are deciding whether to restart. The output is a restart small enough to actually happen this week and a named change to whatever broke it. It is not the routine itself. If the user needs the training programme, meal plan, or study schedule, build that separately once the restart is agreed, or hand off to whichever skill owns it.

Most of what people need here is not a plan. It is permission to restart at a size that feels embarrassingly small, and a reason to believe the restart is not just the same attempt again.

## Before anything: check what kind of thing broke

Read the request for these, and stop the normal procedure if you find one:

- **Substance use.** A broken period of sobriety or reduced drinking is not a broken streak. Say plainly that this is outside what a habit plan can carry, and point at their existing support, a doctor, or a recovery service. Do not build a restart ladder for it, and do not moralise on the way past.
- **Eating, weight, or food rules.** The signals are rules that have got stricter over time, secrecy, compensating for food with exercise or restriction, distress that scales with the number rather than with how they feel, or a target weight defended rather than explained. Those need a professional, and the gentle version of this skill still supplies structure to something that would be better off with less of it.

  Be careful not to over-read this one. Ordinary unhappiness about a changed body is not a clinical signal, and refusing to help a person who mentions weight in passing is its own failure: it reads as being told they are too fragile for the thing they asked for, and it leaves them with nothing. The workable middle is to decline the framing and keep the plan. Do not build the restart around a weight target, say plainly why not — the first month of any physical habit moves the scale very little, so a plan scored on weight looks like it is failing at exactly the point it is working — and then give them the plan they asked for. One sentence declining the frame, no argument about how they should feel, and on to the work.
- **Exercise the user cannot stop rather than cannot start.** Someone distressed about missing sessions while injured, ill, or exhausted is not describing a lapsed habit.
- **Medication or a clinical routine.** Missed doses go to the prescriber, not to a habit ladder.

In those cases, answer the human part in a sentence or two, name why this is not the right tool, and offer the one thing that is. That is a better answer than a plan, not a smaller one.

Otherwise, continue.

## When you only get one message

**This section overrides the instructions to ask, everywhere below.** Several steps are written as questions because they are the right questions when there is a conversation to have. When there is not, do not ask them; answer them yourself from the message and say that you did.

This is usually a single message ending in "just give me a plan", and interviewing in reply is the wrong answer to that. Someone who has been putting off restarting for months will not be helped by being asked six questions before anything happens.

So commit to the most probable cause from what they gave you, say which cause you have assumed and in one line why, and write the plan on it. Then hand them the check: name the one observation that would mean you picked wrong, and what changes if so. A stated assumption the user can overturn in a sentence is worth more than a question that stalls the answer for a day.

Where two causes are genuinely close and lead to different plans, give the branch rather than the average: if the break points share a common event, it is a context change and the anchor is the fix; if they do not, it is the other thing. Two short branches beat one hedged plan.

## Step 1: Size the break honestly, and usually smaller than the user has

People arrive at this conversation having already decided they have failed. Check that before accepting it, because the framing changes the whole plan.

- **A miss.** One or two skipped sessions inside an otherwise intact pattern. This is not a relapse and does not need a recovery plan. Say so, say the next scheduled session is the plan, and stop. Running a full procedure on a single missed gym day teaches the user that a miss is a crisis, which is the belief that turns misses into relapses.
- **A wobble.** A week or two off, with the intention intact and the context unchanged. Restart near where they left off; the main work is removing whatever piece of friction appeared.
- **A break.** A month or more, or a pattern of stopping and restarting. This is the case the rest of this skill is for.
- **A deliberate stop.** Sometimes the habit was dropped for a good reason and the user has not given themselves permission to have dropped it. If the thing genuinely no longer fits their life, the honest answer may be to let it go, or to keep a much smaller version. Offer that explicitly. A restart plan for something the user does not actually want is the most common reason the next attempt also fails.

Ask when they last did the thing, and how long they had been doing it before the break. A habit that ran eighteen months and stopped is a fundamentally different case from one that never got past three weeks, and they need opposite plans: the first has a proven pattern to resume and something specific that interrupted it, the second never had a pattern at all and restarting it unchanged just repeats the experiment.

## Step 2: Find what actually broke it, not what they blame

Ask what was happening in the week it stopped. Not "why did you stop", which reliably produces "I got lazy" and is never the answer. Ask what changed.

Sort the cause into one of these, because the fix differs completely:

- **A context change.** A move, a new job, a schedule shift, a season, a broken car, a gym closing, a child's timetable. The old habit was attached to a cue or a slot that no longer exists. Nothing about the person changed and no amount of discipline restores it. The habit needs re-attaching to something in the current life, and this is by a wide margin the most common cause.
- **A one-off disruption that never ended.** Illness, travel, a deadline, a crisis. The disruption is over but the habit never resumed, because nothing prompted it to and the return grew more daunting the longer it sat.
- **The plan was too big.** It worked while motivation was high and had no version that survived a bad week. The tell is a plan with no defined minimum, so any day that is not a full session is a failed day.
- **The habit had no payoff the user could feel.** Common for anything whose benefit arrives in months or is invisible by design, such as prescribed rehab exercises, flossing, or saving. Without something felt in the meantime the habit runs on willpower alone, and willpower is a poor substitute for a reason.

  The repair is to install a payoff that lands inside the first month, and to pick exactly one rather than offering a menu the user will not choose from. Three shapes work. A **near-term finish line**: one concrete, completable thing with a date on it, small enough to be reached in about four weeks, so there is an event rather than an open-ended slope. A **visible record**: a log, a photo, a rough recording, kept privately, whose only job is to be evidence against the user's memory of how bad they were at the start. A **low-commitment social outlet**: one occasion where the thing goes somewhere, deliberately smaller than the arrangement that broke, so it does not recreate the dependency that caused the break. Ask which of the three fits and put a date on it in the plan.
- **The tracking itself is what breaks.** The habit is fine and the scoring is hostile. Anything measured as an unbroken run — a streak counter, a ring to close, a leaderboard, a chain not to break — converts one ordinary missed day into a total loss, and the reset is far more demoralising than starting from nothing ever was, because the user watches real work get scored as zero. The tell is a user who describes the same cycle several times with the same number in it. Nothing is wrong with them; the metric is doing exactly what it was designed to do and the design assumes nobody ever has a bad week.

  The repair is to replace the metric rather than to try harder against it. Swap the unbreakable run for a count that a miss cannot zero — sessions this month against a target, where missing one moves seventeen out of thirty to seventeen out of twenty-nine, which is a fact rather than a verdict. Turn the streak display off where the app allows it. And pre-commit the rule for the day after a miss, now, while nothing has been missed: the next session is the minimum version, never a double.

- **The habit was someone else's idea.** A goal adopted from a partner, a doctor, a trend, or a version of themselves they think they should be. These break repeatedly and no plan fixes them, because the problem is the goal.
- **Something else got worse.** Sleep, mood, workload, health, a relationship. When the habit is one of several things that stopped at once, the habit is a symptom and restarting it in isolation will not hold. Say so, and ask whether the bigger thing is being handled.

Name the cause explicitly. Where there is a conversation, confirm it with the user; where there is not, state it as an assumption and give them the observation that would overturn it. Everything after this depends on it, and a plan built on "I lost motivation" is a plan built on nothing.

## Step 3: Set the restart size by the gap and the cause, not by a fixed fraction

Do not apply a standard reduction. What governs the restart size is different for different kinds of habit, and using one rule for all of them is how a restart plan becomes an injury or a second abandonment.

- **For anything physical, the body's actual detraining governs.** It is not symmetric with time off and it is not linear: cardiovascular fitness fades noticeably within a few weeks, strength holds longer, tendons and connective tissue recondition more slowly than muscle and are the usual source of a restart injury. The practical consequence is that the first sessions back should be governed by tissue tolerance rather than by how the effort feels, because effort tolerance returns before tissue tolerance does and the user will feel able to do more than they should. Where the break was caused by a physical event rather than merely coinciding with one — surgery, childbirth, a long illness, a significant change in weight either way — the body restarting is not the body that stopped, and time off is the wrong variable to reason from. Say what the medical checkpoint is before the plan rather than after it, name the specific signs that mean see someone first, and keep it to a few lines so it reads as a gate rather than a warning label. Then give the plan. Sending someone away with only a caution is not caution, it is a non-answer.
- **For skill habits — an instrument, a language, writing, study — the loss is mostly access rather than ability.** The material comes back much faster than it went in. Restart near the old level and expect a few frustrating sessions, and say that in advance, because the gap between remembered ability and current ability is the thing that makes people quit in week one.
- **For pure-consistency habits — journalling, flossing, tidying, checking in on someone — there is no detraining at all.** The only thing that decayed is the cue. Restart at full size, and spend the effort on rebuilding the trigger instead.

Having refused the fixed fraction, replace it with a method rather than leaving a hole. Progress one variable at a time, in order: first frequency, until the slot is proven; then duration; then intensity last. Advance only when the previous week actually happened as written, and when a week is missed, repeat that week rather than skipping ahead. That rule works for a return to running, an instrument, and a study habit alike, and unlike a percentage it cannot be applied to the wrong body.

Then, whatever the type, make the first week smaller than the user's estimate of what they can manage. The first week is not training, and it is not catching up. Its only job is to prove the slot exists in the current schedule. A first week that is too easy to fail costs a few days of progress; a first week that is missed costs the whole restart, because a failed restart makes the next one harder to begin.

Define a **minimum version** that counts as a success on the worst plausible day: the five-minute one, the one done at home, the one done badly. Write it down as an explicit part of the plan rather than an exception to it. This single item does more than the rest of the plan combined, because the habit does not break on the good weeks.

Never prescribe making up missed work. Doubling to compensate is the most reliable way to break the restart in its second week, and for physical habits it is also how people get hurt.

## Step 4: Rebuild the trigger, because that is usually the real repair

A habit that has broken has almost always lost its cue rather than its motivation, so this step matters more than the plan's contents.

- Attach it to something that already happens reliably in the user's current week, not the one they used to have. Anchor to an existing fixed event rather than to a clock time, when there is one available.
- Cut the friction before the first rep, which is where habits actually die: the bag not packed, the shoes in a cupboard, the app logged out, the instrument in its case. Ask what happens in the ninety seconds before the habit starts and remove one obstacle from that window.
- Decide in advance what the habit does on the days it cannot happen as planned, since those days are what broke it last time.
- Choose whether the restart is private or announced. Some people need the accountability, and for others the announcement itself becomes a reason not to start. Ask rather than assuming, and note that a person who has restarted publicly several times before may find the audience is now the obstacle.

If the cause in step 2 was a context change, this is the entire fix. Say so, and do not pad the plan around it.

## Step 5: Deal with the guilt, briefly and without a lecture

The user is usually harder on themselves than the situation warrants, and the self-criticism is not harmless: it is a good predictor of a longer gap, because the shame of restarting grows with the gap it is attached to.

Say the useful true thing rather than a reassurance:

- The break is the normal shape of a long-run habit, not the exception. Nobody's real chart is a straight line.
- The time off is not lost. The prior period built something that a first-timer does not have, and the restart is genuinely easier than the original start even when it does not feel like it.
- If they have asked whether they are too old, or whether there is any point at their age, answer it rather than stepping around it. For most habits age changes the rate and the recovery, not the possibility, and the specific adjustments it demands are worth naming plainly. Then say what is actually carrying the question, which is usually that the thing broke when a life stage ended and nothing replaced the structure it used to sit inside. That is a scheduling problem wearing an age costume, and it is fixable.
- The streak was never the point, and a streak-based framing guarantees this conversation happens again, since every streak eventually ends and an all-or-nothing rule turns one missed day into a month.

Keep this to a few lines. A long compassionate passage reads as a consolation prize and delays the part that helps, which is the plan.

## Step 6: Deliver

Give back, briefly:

1. What broke it, in one line, named as a cause rather than a character flaw
2. The first week, concretely: what, when, anchored to what
3. The minimum version that still counts
4. The one change that makes this different from the last attempt
5. A check-in point, usually two to four weeks out, with what would count as it working

Make item 4 explicit and singular. If the plan contains no change from the last attempt, say so honestly rather than presenting the same plan with more encouragement.

Set the check-in on something specific: whether the slot survived contact with a bad week, not whether they feel motivated. Motivation at two weeks predicts nothing.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Treating a single missed day as a relapse and running a full recovery procedure on it, which teaches the user that misses are crises.
- Prescribing a fixed percentage cut for every kind of habit, so a language learner is told to restart at half and a runner is sent back too fast for their tendons.
- Accepting "I lost motivation" as the cause. It is a description of the symptom and the plan built on it will be the same plan that already failed.
- Rebuilding the plan while ignoring that the cue is gone, when the missing cue was the whole problem.
- Prescribing catch-up work to make up for lost time.
- Restarting a habit the user has no actual reason to want, because they did not feel allowed to say so.
- Treating a habit that stopped alongside sleep, mood, and work as an isolated discipline problem.
- Long paragraphs of reassurance in place of a plan, or a plan with no acknowledgement that the user feels bad. Both fail, in opposite directions.
- Running this at all on substance use, disordered eating, or compulsive exercise, where the structure this skill supplies is the wrong intervention.
- Firing the same gate on someone who simply mentioned their weight in passing, and handing back a referral instead of the plan they asked for.
- Interviewing a user who asked for a plan, when naming the assumed cause and the way to check it would have got them started today.
- Diagnosing a missing payoff and then not installing one, so the plan is correct about why the habit died and does nothing about it.
- Leaving a hostile metric in place and adding willpower on top of it, so the next missed day resets the score to zero again.
