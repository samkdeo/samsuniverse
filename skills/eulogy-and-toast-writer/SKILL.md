# Eulogy and Toast Writer

Produces a speech the speaker can actually deliver: written for their voice, sized to the slot, and built out of specific things that happened rather than adjectives. The failure state is a fluent, warm, entirely interchangeable speech that could be given about almost anyone, and everything below exists to avoid it.

It does not decide what the speaker feels or tell them what they ought to say about someone. Where there is real difficulty in the relationship, the job is to help them say something true that they can live with afterwards, not to smooth it into something false.

## Start here, before any procedure

Two things decide almost everything, so get them first, and if the user has not said, assume and label rather than stalling.

- **The occasion and the slot.** What kind of speech, how long, where in the running order, and who else is speaking. A eulogy that follows three others needs different material from the only one. A toast before the meal is a different animal from one after it.
- **The speaker's relationship**, in one line, and whether the room knows the speaker. A speech from someone the room does not know needs to establish standing in a sentence; one from a sibling does not.

Then, if this is a funeral or the person is recently bereaved, adjust how you ask for everything else. Do not open with a questionnaire. Ask for one story, then work outward from it. Someone three days into a bereavement can often produce a specific memory and cannot produce a structured account of a life, and demanding the second gets you nothing usable and makes the conversation worse.

## Length is a time, not a word count, and it governs everything

Get the time first, because it determines how much material can exist and every later decision hangs on it.

Do not convert the slot into a word count. A word count is the wrong instrument for these speeches specifically, because what fills the time is not the words: it is the pauses, the laughter, the breath in the hard paragraph, and a delivery slower than ordinary speech. Two speeches of identical length run a minute apart depending on how much of either they contain, and a rate that fits a best man's fluent patter will badly overrun a eulogy.

So time it rather than counting it, and put the method in the speaker's hands. Read the draft aloud, standing, at the pace it will actually be delivered, with a stopwatch running. Then add a margin, because a room only ever adds time, never removes it: laughter has to be waited out, and grief has to be breathed through. Draft to the lower end of the slot for the same reason.

This also has to be done by the speaker rather than by you, since their pace is the one that matters, and it doubles as the rehearsal that finds the sentences they cannot say.

If nobody has told the speaker how long, that is a question worth them asking, and in its absence assume the short end. Nobody has ever complained that a speech at an emotional occasion was too short.

The practical consequence is subtractive. A tight slot does not mean compressing everything said into shorter sentences; it means carrying fewer things, fully. One story told properly beats three summarised, and the instinct to include every important person or every phase of a life is what produces a list that honours nobody.

## Step 1: Collect specifics, not adjectives

The interview is the whole job. A speech is only as good as the material, and the material people offer first is always the abstract layer: kind, generous, always there for everyone, larger than life. Those are conclusions. The speech needs the evidence that produced them.

So ask for concrete things, in this shape:

- **A scene.** A moment with a place and a time in it. Where were you, what happened, what was said.
- **A habit or a repeated thing.** The phrase they always used, the way they answered the phone, what they always did on a Sunday, the thing they were absurdly particular about. Repeated details land harder than singular ones because the room recognises them, and half the audience will have their own version.
- **A specific object.** The car, the coat, the chair, the tool, the recipe. Objects carry more than descriptions do.
- **A thing they were wrong about, or bad at, or funny about.** This is what separates a real person from a plaque. Handled with warmth, an acknowledged flaw is what makes the praise credible, and its absence is why generic tributes feel hollow.
- **Something the speaker learned from them, or the thing they would say about this exact situation.**
- **What the room already knows**, so the speech does not spend its length on biography everyone has.

When someone gives you an adjective, ask for the occasion that proves it. "He was generous" becomes a speech when it becomes the time he did the specific thing. Do this every time, gently, and do not accept the abstraction as material.

## Drafting when there is no interview

What decides between drafting and interviewing is the time before the occasion, not the thinness of the message. With weeks in hand, hand back the questions and wait for real material, because a draft built on placeholders will be treated as finished and the specifics will never arrive. With days in hand, draft.

Very often there is one message, thin material, and a date close enough that homework is not an option. The interview above is still the right procedure; it just has to be run differently.

Draft anyway, and make the gaps visible and specific. Put a bracketed placeholder where a concrete detail belongs, written as a guess at the *shape* of the thing rather than as a fabricated fact: the object he would have turned up with, the phrase she always used, the number of years. A bracket that names what belongs there gets filled. A finished-looking sentence containing an invented detail is far worse, because a speaker under pressure will read it out, and it will be the one sentence in the speech that somebody in the room knows is not true.

Never invent a specific: not a quote, not a place, not an anecdote, not a number. Everything invented must be visibly bracketed, and say so plainly at the top of the draft rather than only at the bottom.

Then say what you assumed — the relationship, the vocabulary, the register — and offer the through-line for correction before the draft rather than after it. Where the material is genuinely too thin to draft, ask for the smallest number of specifics that would unlock it, framed as the questions in step 1 rather than as a general request for more.

## Step 2: Find the through-line before writing anything

A good short speech is about one thing. A weak one is a list of nice qualities in no order, and it is the default outcome when someone drafts straight from a pile of memories.

Read the material and find the single claim the speech is making: what this person was like, in a way the stories all evidence. Then keep only the material that serves it. Two or three stories that point the same direction beat six that point everywhere, and the discarded ones are not wasted, since they belong in conversation afterwards and it is worth saying so to a speaker reluctant to cut.

State the through-line to the user in a sentence before drafting, so they can correct it. If it is wrong, everything downstream is wrong, and the person who knew the deceased will spot it instantly.

## Step 3: Structure

The shape that works for almost all of these:

1. **An opening that earns attention in one or two sentences.** Not "when I was asked to speak today". Start inside a story, with a line of speech, or with the single concrete detail everyone in the room will recognise. The weakest opening available is an announcement that you are about to speak, followed by the speech.
2. **Standing**, in one line, and only if the room needs it. Who the speaker is to the subject.
3. **The stories**, in an order that builds rather than a chronology. Chronology is the default and it is usually the wrong choice, because a life told in order peaks in the middle and ends in the hardest material.
4. **The turn**, where the specifics become the thing being said about the person. One or two sentences. This is the load-bearing part and it should be the plainest language in the speech.
5. **The close**, which lands and stops.

For a toast, the close is the toast itself, and the room needs to know it is coming so it can lift its glasses. Give the physical cue in the text.

For a eulogy, the close is where speakers most often overreach. It does not need to resolve a death or find a meaning in it. Ending on a small, specific, true thing is stronger than ending on the largest available sentiment, and speakers who reach for the big statement usually do it because the small one felt insufficient. It is not.

## Step 4: Write it to be spoken

The draft has to survive being said aloud by a nervous person.

- Short sentences. A sentence that needs a breath in the middle will be delivered badly.
- Write it in the speaker's own vocabulary. If they say "mum" do not write "mother"; if they do not swear, do not put swearing in; if they are funny in a dry way, do not write jokes that need performing. A speech in the wrong register is the most common thing a speaker cannot deliver, and they will find that out at the lectern.
- No rhetorical constructions the speaker would never use. Rules of three, elaborate parallelism, and rhetorical questions read well and sound written, which is the one thing a speech at an emotional occasion must not sound.
- Say the words that people avoid. In a eulogy, "died" is a kinder word than "passed", because the euphemism asks the room to look away and the plain word lets them stay. Follow the family's own usage if they have one, but do not reach for the euphemism by default.
- Mark where the pauses are, and mark the places likely to be hard to get through, because those need a planned breath rather than an improvised one.
- Read the whole thing aloud at pace before delivering the final version. This is instruction for the user, not for you, and it should be in the handover.

## What is different about a toast

The eulogy guidance above transfers, but four things change and they are what toasts get wrong.

**The subject is in the room and can respond.** Everything said is said to their face, in front of their new family, their colleagues, and whoever they most want to look competent in front of. It is also usually recorded. The joke that works between two friends does not automatically work when one of them cannot answer back for twenty minutes.

**Calibrate the roasting by what the affection can carry.** Some warmth-with-teeth is what makes a toast land, and a speech with none reads as a reference letter. The working test is whether the subject would happily have it said about them by someone who loves them, in that room, on that day. Anything touching sex, exes, drink, money, or an old humiliation fails that test far more often than the speaker believes, and the speaker is the worst-placed person in the room to judge it, because they know exactly how they mean it and nobody else does. Offer the version that keeps the affection and drops the exposure rather than simply vetoing it.

**A tease has to be cashed.** A line promising stories that cannot be told promises dirt and then delivers none, which leaves half the room braced for something that never arrives. Either tell a version of the story or drop the tease.

**Watch the running order and the balance of names.** Other speakers will use up material, so find out who is speaking and roughly what they will cover. And a speech for a couple that is entirely about the one the speaker knows is the standard own goal: the other person should be a real presence in it rather than a name at the end.

**Give the physical cue for the toast itself**, so the room knows to lift its glasses, and stop immediately after it.

## Step 5: Check the room

Run the draft against the specific audience before calling it done.

- **Who is in the room who will hear this differently.** A former partner, an estranged relative, children, colleagues, someone who was recently in the same position, the person whose name is not mentioned. A line that plays as affectionate to half the room and pointed to the other half is the failure that gets remembered.
- **The story that belongs to someone else.** If the material involves a third party's private business, illness, or reputation, the speaker is disclosing on their behalf in a room they cannot leave. Check it.
- **Names.** Get every name right and get the pronunciation noted. Include the people whose absence would be noticed, in a way that does not turn into a list.
- **In a eulogy, whether the family has asked for anything to be avoided.** Ask, if it has not been said.

## Step 6: Deliver the speech and the practical notes

Hand back the speech itself, formatted for reading aloud rather than for the page: short paragraphs, a line break where the pause goes, no dense blocks.

Then a short set of notes:

- The run time, and where to cut if it overruns
- The two or three places to slow down
- The plan for the moment when they cannot speak, which for a eulogy is worth writing down: it is normal, the room is entirely with them, the fix is to stop and breathe rather than to push on, and it is fine to have someone briefed to step in and finish. Knowing that is available makes it less likely to be needed.
- What to have on paper. Full text, large type, on cards that do not flap, numbered in case they are dropped. Nobody should be reading a speech from a phone at a funeral.

## When the difficulty is the room rather than the person

Most workplace speeches and a fair number of family ones are hard for reasons that have nothing to do with the speaker's feelings about the subject. Someone is leaving involuntarily. A redundancy round is fresh. A retirement was not really chosen. Part of the room is relieved and part is angry, and both halves will listen for whose side the speaker is on. The relationship may be perfectly warm; the occasion is the problem.

The rules that hold across all of these:

- **Do not take a side, and do not hint at one.** A wry pause, a loaded emphasis, or a defence offered on the subject's behalf all take a side, and a speech that takes one turns the subject into ammunition at their own leaving do. Neutrality here is not cowardice; it is the only version that does not make the day worse for the person it is about.
- **Say something true about the person and nothing at all about the process.** The through-line should be a claim that stays true regardless of how this ended, which is what makes it sayable in front of a divided room.
- **One line can acknowledge the situation, and it works precisely because it does not elaborate.** Something to the effect that today is not landing the same way for everyone, and that is fair enough. Do not add the clause explaining what you mean, because that clause is where the side-taking happens.
- **Do not predict the future.** Telling someone this will turn out to be the best thing that ever happened to them, when they did not choose it, is the sentence that sounds most like the management that made the decision.
- **Do not thank the institution.** Gratitude that is not personally the speaker's reads as corporate, and in this room it reads as a defence.
- **Ask the subject beforehand what they would rather was not said.** They may want the room to treat it as an ordinary occasion, or they may want something named. This costs one message and removes all the guessing, and it applies to any occasion where the subject is present and the circumstances are awkward.
- **Watch the speaker's own standing.** A speaker who is part of the structure that produced the situation is heard differently from a peer, and warmth from that position can read as management smoothing things over. Plainer is safer.

## The difficult version, where the relationship was the problem

Sometimes the person was difficult, the relationship was bad, or the speaker is angry as well as sad. This is common and the standard procedure makes it worse by asking for warm stories that do not exist.

Say plainly that they do not have to lie and do not have to perform a closeness that was not there. Then find the register that is true and survivable: what the person made, what they were good at, what others got from them, what is honestly complicated said once and without detail. "He was not an easy man, and he was my father" is a sentence a room can hold. A speech that overclaims will be heard as false by everyone who knew, and the speaker has to live with having said it.

Where the honest answer is that they should not give the speech, that is a legitimate outcome and worth naming.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- A fluent speech of admirable qualities that would fit any decent person, because the adjectives were never converted into events.
- Chronology as structure, so the speech peaks in the middle and trails into the hardest part of a life.
- Writing in the model's register rather than the speaker's, producing something they cannot say out loud.
- Rhetorical questions, rules of three, and elaborate parallelism, which sound written.
- Overrunning, because the draft was timed at reading pace and the room adds pauses and laughter.
- Trying to include every relative and every phase, which converts the speech into a list.
- Reaching for the biggest possible closing sentiment when a small specific one would land harder.
- A joke that is funny to the speaker and exposing to the subject, in a room the subject cannot leave.
- Demanding a structured life history from someone recently bereaved, when one story would have opened the whole thing.
- Making a difficult relationship sound warm, which the room hears as false and the speaker has to carry afterwards.
- Inventing a specific detail to make a draft read as finished, which a speaker under pressure will deliver as fact.
- Converting a slot into a word count, which measures the wrong thing entirely for a speech built out of pauses.
- Writing a toast for a subject who is not there, when they are sitting six feet away and cannot reply.
- Reading a hard occasion as a hard relationship, and reaching for warmth when the actual problem is that half the room is angry.
- Predicting that an involuntary ending will turn out for the best, in front of the person it happened to.
