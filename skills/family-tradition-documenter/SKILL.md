# Family Tradition Documenter

Produces a record of something a family does or knows, in the words of the person who holds it. The output is a document that sounds like them and contains the details nobody thought to mention, not a neat summary that could describe anyone's family.

The premise: what gets lost is never the outline. Everyone remembers that the family went to the coast every August. What goes is the specific — which beach, whose car, the argument about the route, the thing that was always said when they arrived — and those details are only available from someone who was there, and only while they are here.

It captures. It does not verify, tidy, or resolve. It is not genealogy, and where the user's question is really about records and dates, say so and point them elsewhere.

## Start with what is most at risk

Ask what prompted this, because urgency changes the order completely.

Where someone is ill, elderly, or the family has recently had a scare, do not build a project. Start recording this week, badly, with whatever is to hand, on the single most irreplaceable thing. A phone voice memo of a twenty-minute conversation is worth more than a beautifully planned interview that has not happened, and the planned version has a habit of never happening.

Order by what is genuinely unrecoverable:

- **What only one living person knows.** This is everything. Anything held by one person, with no document and nobody else who was there, is the whole priority.
- **What a photograph or an object cannot explain by itself.** Who these people are, where this was, what was happening, why it was kept. An unlabelled photograph is a mystery within one generation.
- **Practical knowledge held in the hands** — a recipe nobody has written down, a technique, a repair, a way of doing something. These vanish completely, because they were never in words in the first place.
- **What is embarrassing, difficult, or was never discussed**, which needs the most care and disappears fastest, because nobody wants to be the one to ask.

Say the honest thing about timing if it applies: the reason people regret this is almost never that they recorded the wrong thing, it is that they waited.

## When the person's memory is already going

Frequently the reason someone has started, and the file above quietly assumes an intact narrator. Several things change.

**Record anyway, and record sooner.** Long-term memory often outlasts recent memory by years, so an account of sixty years ago may be vivid while yesterday is gone. What is being captured is their account on that day, which is what it always was.

**Do not correct.** Contradicting a detail interrupts the telling, achieves nothing, and can distress someone who cannot follow why they are being disputed. Note the discrepancy in your own record instead, with the date, and keep both versions.

**Consent is a repeated act rather than a single one.** Ask at the start of each session in plain terms, watch for discomfort rather than relying on the earlier yes, and stop when they want to stop. Where capacity is genuinely in doubt, involve whoever else holds responsibility, and keep the purpose modest and family-facing rather than public.

**Shorter and more often.** Twenty minutes several times beats one long session, and this is better data as well as kinder.

**Expect repetition, and let it happen.** A story told three times in a week with different details is three records, not one failure.

**Date everything.** With a changing narrator the date on a recording stops being administrative and becomes part of what the record means.

## When the tradition happens at a fixed time of year

Common, and worth catching early because it changes the plan rather than the technique: the thing being documented happens in one particular month and the visit is in a different one.

Practical knowledge cannot be captured by asking about it out of season, because the answer will be a summary and the summary is exactly what is wrong. So say plainly that being present for the doing is the requirement, and treat the trip that coincides with it as the priority even if it is short. A single day on the right day beats a week on the wrong one.

Where that is genuinely impossible, the fallback is not a dictated account. It is to get their hands busy on something adjacent — cooking anything, sorting the equipment, walking through the place where it happens — and talk about the real thing while they work. People say far more alongside a task than across a table, and standing in the actual kitchen pointing at the actual pan retrieves what a question cannot.

Use the out-of-season visit to set up the in-season one: ask to be taught, rather than asking to be told.

## When it is a first meeting rather than a family archive

Sometimes the person holding the account is not an established relative: a birth parent found late, a relative long estranged, someone who knew a family member and is being approached cold. The method transfers almost intact and one thing inverts, so it has to be said.

**The urgency bias above is wrong here.** Everywhere else the risk is waiting too long. Here the risk is asking too much, because the relationship is what determines whether there is a second meeting, and there almost always could be one. Someone who leaves feeling met will see you again; someone who leaves feeling debriefed may not. When it comes to a choice between the last few questions and simply spending the time together, spend the time. Say this plainly, because the person's fear of never getting another chance is exactly what causes them to lose it.

**Take a pre-declared limit at face value.** Where someone has said in advance that they will answer what they can, they are being honest rather than evasive. Ask the hard things once, late rather than early, plainly, and then accept the answer without a follow-up. Saying that they do not have to answer, and meaning it, is what buys the next conversation.

**The interviewer has a stake here**, which they do not in an ordinary family recording, and it changes the discipline. Do not correct, do not cross-examine, and do not try to resolve a discrepancy between what is being said and what the person was told elsewhere. Note it privately with the date and keep both. Arguing a detail ends the telling and gains nothing.

**Triage for a single meeting** by what only this person holds: their own account, which no document contains; anything with practical consequences that cannot be obtained elsewhere; and the map of who else exists and could ever be asked, since one person is often the door to everyone else and some of them are older. Photographs and objects, if there are any, are the highest-yield hour available and only they can annotate them.

## Record, do not transcribe

The single most consequential decision, and people get it backwards.

Capture audio or video first, always, even if the plan is a written document. The recording holds the voice, the pauses, the laughter, the way they say a particular word, and the fact that someone got upset in a specific place. All of that is data and none of it survives transcription. Families listen back to voices decades later; they very rarely reread a tidy paragraph.

Practical points that matter more than equipment quality:

- **Any recorder is fine.** The phone in their pocket is fine. Nobody has ever regretted the audio quality of a recording of a parent's voice; plenty regret not having one.
- **Get consent, plainly**, and say what it is for and who will hear it. This matters more than it sounds, both for the person and for the family later.
- **Record more than the interview.** The unguarded ten minutes afterwards, when the notebook is closed, is frequently where the real thing is said.
- **Say the date, the place, and who is present at the start of the recording.** Trivial, takes five seconds, and is the difference between a usable archive and a pile of unlabelled files.
- **Back it up before anything else**, in more than one place. A single copy of an irreplaceable recording is the largest avoidable risk in this whole exercise.

## Ask the questions that produce detail

The direct question fails. "Tell me about your childhood" produces an outline, because it asks for a summary and people summarise when asked to.

What works is asking about the specific, the sensory, and the ordinary:

- **Ask about a single day rather than a period.** Not what Christmas was like, but the Christmas something happened, or simply the last one before something changed.
- **Ask what things cost, what they smelled like, what the room was like, what was on the radio.** Sensory and concrete questions retrieve far more than abstract ones, and they unlock adjacent memories the person did not know they still had.
- **Ask about the ordinary rather than the momentous.** What did they eat on a normal Tuesday, who did the washing, what was the walk to school. People assume nobody wants to know this and it is the material that turns out to matter most, because it is the part that is genuinely gone.
- **Ask about objects.** Hand them something and ask about it. An object in the hand produces more than any question asked across a table.
- **Ask who taught them.** Traditions and skills come from someone, and that person is often the earliest thing anyone can reach.
- **Ask what changed, and when it stopped.** Traditions have an end as well as a beginning, and the reason something stopped is usually the most revealing part.
- **Ask what they were not allowed to talk about**, when the moment is right and the relationship carries it.
- **Ask the same question again months later.** Accounts differ between tellings, and the differences are information rather than errors.

Follow the tangent. The tangent is nearly always better than the question, and an interviewer who steers back to their list will get the list and nothing else.

Leave silence. People fill it, and what they fill it with is usually the thing they had decided not to say. This is the single most useful interviewing habit available and it is the hardest to do.

## When another family member objects

Common, and usually voiced as protecting the person from being made a fuss of. Do not treat it as an obstacle to be routed around, because the objector often has genuine standing — particularly where the subject's health or capacity is changing — and going behind them turns one conversation into two problems.

What defuses it is scale and framing. An announced project sounds like a documentary; asking to be taught something, with a phone on the table, does not, and it is also the truer description. Invite the objector to be present rather than excluding them. And offer to play them the result afterwards: people who object in the abstract generally stop once they have heard ten minutes of an ordinary conversation in a voice they love.

Where the objection persists, do the smallest version rather than winning the argument. A recording that exists is worth more than a plan that is still being negotiated.

## Getting a reluctant person to talk

Common, and worth handling directly rather than treating as a failure.

- **Do not call it an interview.** The framing makes people perform, and performance is the enemy of the specific. It is a chat, and the recorder is on the table rather than pointed at them.
- **Do not start with the hardest thing.** Start where they are comfortable and competent — what they knew how to do, what they were good at.
- **Use an activity.** Cooking the thing, looking through a box, driving past the old house, sorting photographs. Talk that happens alongside a task is far more forthcoming than talk that is the task, and people who dislike being interviewed will happily talk for two hours while doing something.
- **Ask them to teach you.** This reframes them from subject to expert and it works on almost everyone, particularly with practical knowledge.
- **Accept short sessions.** Several twenty-minute conversations beat one exhausting afternoon, especially where health or attention is a factor.
- **Where they refuse, respect it.** Someone entitled to their own privacy does not owe the family an account of themselves, and a record extracted against someone's will is both wrong and usually poor. Ask again later, differently, and be prepared to be told no permanently.

## Capturing practical knowledge, which needs a different method

A recipe or a technique held by one person is rarely written down and, when it is, is usually wrong, because the person leaves out everything they consider obvious.

So do not ask them to write it out. Watch them do it and record it, and be specific about the things they will not think to say:

- **Quantities they do by eye**, weighed or measured as they go, by you rather than by them.
- **The judgements**: how they know it is ready, what it should look like, feel like, sound like. This is the actual knowledge and it is the part that never gets written down.
- **What goes wrong, and how they fix it.** Ask directly, because it never appears in a written version.
- **Where the ingredients or materials come from**, and what they do when they cannot get the right one.
- **The step they do without noticing.** Ask an observer to watch for it, since the person doing it genuinely cannot see it.

Set the recording up so it does not need managing. Prop a phone where it sees the hands and the work surface and leave it running for the whole session rather than starting and stopping, since the useful moment is never the one anyone would have chosen to record. A second person is worth a great deal here: one asks and weighs, the other watches. And do the weighing yourself rather than asking them to — someone who has done it by eye for fifty years will resist being slowed down, and the request lands much better as wanting to get it right than as checking up on them.

Then have someone else follow the record while the original person watches. Everything missing surfaces immediately, and this is the only reliable test.

## Write it down in their voice

When it becomes a document, the aim is that it sounds like them.

- **Keep their words.** Their phrasing, their expressions, their names for things, their grammar. Tidying it into standard prose removes the person and leaves an anecdote.
- **Keep the digressions**, or at least some of them. The shape of how someone tells a story is part of what is being preserved.
- **Do not resolve the contradictions.** Where two relatives remember something differently, record both and say who said what. A family archive that has quietly picked a winner has destroyed the more interesting record.
- **Mark what is uncertain**, and separate what someone remembers from what they were told.
- **Attribute everything**, with who said it and when. In twenty years, that is what makes it usable.
- **Do not fabricate the connective tissue.** Where an account has a gap, leave the gap visible rather than smoothing it, because a plausible invention becomes family fact within one generation and cannot be reversed.

## Store it so it survives

A record that only exists on one device or in one account is not preserved.

- Several copies, in different places, including one that is not in the cloud
- Formats that will still open, and plain text or common formats in preference to anything proprietary
- A file naming scheme that says who, what, and when, so a stranger can navigate it
- **Told to somebody.** The most common way this is lost is that one person made the archive and nobody else knew where it was.
- Share it while people are alive to correct it, which improves it and is also the point

## When the person has already gone

Different job, and worth saying plainly that it is a reconstruction rather than a record.

Gather what several people each hold, separately, before they discuss it, since a group conversation converges on one version quickly and the differences are lost. Then bring the accounts together with the disagreements intact.

Work from objects and photographs, which prompt memory better than questions. Look for what was written down incidentally — letters, recipe cards, the backs of photographs, notes in the margins of books, inscriptions — which is where the specifics usually survive. And ask the people slightly outside the family, such as neighbours and old friends, who are routinely never asked and often remember things the family cannot.

Record what cannot be recovered, explicitly. A named gap is more honest and more useful than a smooth account, and it tells the next person where to look.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Planning a project when someone is ill, instead of recording something badly this week.
- Writing a summary instead of recording a voice, which loses everything that made it worth keeping.
- Asking someone to tell you about their childhood, which produces an outline.
- Asking only about the momentous, and losing the ordinary detail that is the part actually gone.
- Steering back to the question list instead of following the tangent.
- Filling the silences.
- Tidying the transcript into good prose, which removes the person.
- Resolving two conflicting accounts into one, which destroys the more valuable record.
- Inventing the connective tissue, which becomes family fact within a generation.
- Asking someone to write out a recipe rather than watching them make it.
- Making the archive and telling nobody where it is.
- Pressing someone who has said no.
- Carrying the urgency of a deathbed recording into a first meeting, where asking too much is what costs you the second one.
- Correcting someone whose memory is failing, or treating a repeated story as a failed one.
- Trying to capture a skill out of season by asking about it, when the answer to that question is always a summary.
