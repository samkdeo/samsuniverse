# Travel Packing by Trip Type

Produces a list for one specific trip, built from what that trip actually contains. A generic list is worse than none, because it is long enough to feel authoritative and it will still miss the two things that matter, which are almost always the item that cannot be bought at the destination and the item that only one activity on the itinerary needs.

It does not plan the trip. It does not rule on visas, vaccinations, medication import rules, or what customs will allow, all of which change and should be checked against an official source; where one of those is clearly relevant, say so and say where to check, rather than guessing.

## The escape hatch

If someone is going somewhere familiar, for a couple of nights, alone or as two adults, with no unusual activity, they do not need a procedure. Ask what is different about this trip from the last one and answer that.

Do not read familiarity as simplicity, though. A trip to a relative's house is a small trip for one adult and a large one the moment children, an animal, an occasion, or a fixed volume limit are involved, and those are exactly the trips people describe as routine while packing the entire house. What decides is the number of constraints, not how well the user knows the destination.

Run the full thing when there is a real constraint — a fixed bag or boot, a long trip, several activities, a hard climate, dependants, or something the user cannot replace once they have left.

## Step 1: Get the six things the list is derived from

Everything below depends on these. Almost every real request arrives with three of the five missing, and that is normal rather than a reason to stop: assume, label the assumption, and write the list. A list with its assumptions stated is useful today, and a set of questions is useful tomorrow at best.

Grade the assumptions rather than listing them flatly, because they are not equally dangerous.

- **Cheap to get wrong**, meaning the user can check it later and adjust by an item or two: the weather, the dress code, whether a particular thing is buyable there. State these and move on.
- **Expensive to get wrong**, meaning the whole plan changes: the bag allowance and whether a cabin bag is actually included in the fare, whether there is laundry, and whether anyone else on the trip can carry things. Put these at the top, tell the user to check them specifically, and say what changes in each direction.

Getting the laundry assumption wrong changes the clothing count by a factor. Getting the weather wrong changes one layer.

1. **The activity mix**, in blocks. Not "a holiday in Spain" but the actual set: three days of meetings, two days walking, one dinner with a dress code, a day at a pool. Each block has its own kit and this is what a generic list cannot know.
2. **The real weather**, not the seasonal average. Ask for the forecast if the trip is near enough to have one, and otherwise ask what the range is. What matters is the spread and the specifics rather than the mean: the overnight low, whether it rains, and the local effects that averages hide, such as altitude, coastal wind, air conditioning indoors in hot countries, and unheated buildings in mild ones. A destination with a comfortable daily average and a ten-degree overnight drop needs a layer that the average does not suggest.
3. **The container, and what actually limits it.** Ask how they are travelling before assuming an airline, because the binding constraint differs completely and half of this skill misfires if you get it wrong.

   Flying: the size and weight limit, whether a cabin bag is genuinely included in the fare rather than assumed, whether a personal item counts separately, and whether there are internal or regional legs with tighter limits than the main one. Internal legs are where a bag that was legal on the way out becomes a charge.

   Driving: weight is irrelevant and volume and geometry are everything. The limits are that the boot must shut, the driver must be able to see out of the back, and rigid objects dictate the shape of everything around them. Load rigid and heavy flat on the floor, keep soft bags on top because a holdall takes the shape of the space left over while a hard case dictates it, treat the parcel shelf line as a hard ceiling rather than a target, and use the footwells and the gaps between seats, which are free volume that otherwise goes to waste.

   Train, coach, or boat: the limit is usually what one person can carry in one trip up a set of stairs, and how much of it must be handled at once at a change.

4. **What the accommodation already has.** This is the direct analogue of the laundry question and it is the single largest lever on any trip where someone else's home or a well-equipped rental is involved. One message asking what is already there removes more from a bag than any packing technique, and it removes the bulkiest things specifically: sleeping equipment for a child, a cot or a bed guard, a high chair, towels, animal kit, a hairdryer, sports or beach equipment.

   Ask for it concretely rather than in general. "We have one" often means it is in a loft under other things, so the useful question names the item and asks whether it is accessible this week.
5. **Laundry access.** This is the single biggest determinant of clothing volume and it is the question people never ask themselves.
6. **What is coming back.** Gifts, shopping, conference material, equipment being collected. If the bag is full on the way out it is over on the way back, and this is worth one line at the start rather than a crisis at the airport.

## Step 2: Split the list by whether it can be replaced there

This is the organising principle, and it is what makes the list short. Most packing anxiety attaches to things that could be bought in ten minutes at the destination, while the genuinely unrecoverable items get one line of attention each.

**Cannot be replaced, or not without real cost or delay.** These get checked twice and go in the bag that stays with the traveller:

- Prescription medication, in enough quantity for the trip plus a margin for a delayed return, in its labelled packaging. A repeat prescription obtained abroad ranges from awkward to impossible.
- Documents: passport, visa or entry authorisation, driving permit, insurance details, tickets, and any booking that will be asked for on arrival.
- Anything fitted or prescribed: glasses, contact lenses and their solution, hearing aids, orthotics, a mouthguard, a brace. Add the spare where one exists, since the failure of one of these ends the activity it supports.
- Chargers and cables with the right connector, plus a plug adapter, plus the one proprietary cable that nothing else in the world will charge.
- Keys, cards, and anything else whose absence is discovered at the worst moment.
- Anything specialist and sized: boots that are broken in, a helmet that fits, a device with a specific battery.

**Can be bought there, cheaply, in minutes.** Toiletries, basic clothing, an umbrella, a phone cable, most over-the-counter medicine. Say this explicitly, because naming it is what gives the user permission to stop packing for hypotheticals. Two caveats worth stating: it is not true everywhere, and it is not true at 11pm on arrival day, so the small quantity that covers the first night still travels.

## Step 3: Set clothing volume by the wash cycle, not by the trip length

The instinct is one outfit per day, and it is wrong for anything longer than a few days. What governs is how often the clothes are washed, and that turns a fortnight into roughly the same volume as five days.

So ask about laundry access, and pack to the cycle:

- **No laundry at all**, which is realistic on short trips and on some remote ones: volume is set by trip length, and this is the only case where the day count is the right number.
- **A machine, or a laundry service, or a launderette:** pack enough for one cycle plus what is being worn, plus a margin for the cycle slipping by a day. The trip length stops mattering almost entirely past that point, and a three-week trip needs barely more clothing than a one-week trip.
- **Washing by hand in a sink**, which works for underwear, socks, and technical fabrics and does not work for jeans or anything thick: pack to a shorter cycle, and check that something in the bag actually dries overnight. A cotton item washed in a humid climate will still be wet in the morning, so the fabric choice is the thing that makes this plan work or fail.

Then count the layers rather than the outfits, because a layered system re-wears far better than a set of complete outfits and is what makes a small bag work across a temperature range. The items that touch the skin are washed often and packed in quantity; the items over the top are worn repeatedly and packed once or twice.

Say the number for each category rather than writing "enough". "Enough socks" is what a generic list says and it is what people get wrong.

## Step 4: Work the activity blocks, and check what is rentable

Take each block from step 1 and write its kit as a group, then ask one question of each group: is this available at the destination?

A large share of packing weight is equipment that is rented or provided where the user is going, and the answer changes the bag completely. Bulky sports and outdoor equipment is very often rentable and sometimes must be. Formal wear is frequently hireable. Some things must be personal — anything sized to the body, anything worn against the skin, anything whose fit is a safety matter — and those travel whatever the weight.

Then find the **single-use heavy items**, which is where overpacking actually happens. Not the extra t-shirt but the one bulky object carried for one possible occasion: the boots for a walk that might not happen, the outfit for one dinner, the equipment for the day that is weather-dependent. For each, name what it costs to carry, what it would cost to rent, buy, or do without, and what happens if the occasion does not occur. Then let the user decide. Some of them are worth it; the point is that the decision is made deliberately rather than by default.

Where one item can serve two blocks, say so, because the cross-block item is the highest-value thing on any list. A shoe or a jacket that works for both the working days and the walking days removes more weight than any amount of trimming elsewhere.

## Step 5: Check the things a list format hides

These are the misses that a well-organised list still produces, because they are not items in a category.

- **Dress codes and local expectations.** A venue with a rule, a religious site with a covering requirement, a workplace more formal than the user's own, a restaurant booking. Ask rather than assume, and note where a single lightweight item covers a requirement that would otherwise mean a separate outfit.
- **The first three hours.** What is needed between landing and reaching the accommodation, and is it in the bag that will be to hand? This is the section that catches the checked-bag mistake: the medication, the layer for a cold arrival, the thing needed to get through the airport.
- **The delayed or lost bag.** If anything is checked, the question is what one day without it looks like. Medication, documents, glasses, and one change of clothes ride in the cabin.
- **Overnight and in-transit.** Long flights, night trains, early starts, and a layover where nothing is open.
- **The return leg's different rules.** Duty-free liquids bought airside, a bag that is now heavier, an internal flight with a smaller allowance, an item that was fine going out and is restricted coming back.
- **Shared items, when travelling with others.** One adapter set, one first aid kit, one hairdryer between two people. Ask who else is going and what is being duplicated, since this is often the easiest weight saving available.
- **Children, if any are on the trip.** On a trip with small children the children are the packing problem rather than a section of it, so work them first rather than last.

  Three things change. **The rigid items are theirs.** Sleeping equipment, a buggy or carrier, a seat, a crate or carrier for an animal: these are the objects that set the size of the load, and each one is worth asking whether the destination already has it or whether a much smaller substitute does the same job. **Consumables scale with accidents rather than with days**, so the underwear and change-of-clothes counts run ahead of the day count in a way the wash cycle does not fix, while the bulky pack of a thing sold in every shop at the destination is exactly the wrong thing to carry. **One item has no substitute and no shop**: the comfort object. It does not go in a bag that is out of reach, it does not get packed in something being checked or strapped to a roof, and if a duplicate exists it comes too.

  The first-three-hours section matters far more with children, and it is usually the arrival that goes wrong rather than the journey: late, dark, everyone tired, shops shut.

## Reviewing a list the user already has

A large share of requests arrive as a written list and "tell me what to cut". Do the derivation in step 1 first anyway — the cuts are not decidable without it — then run three passes rather than one, because a review that only subtracts is half a review.

1. **The absence pass, first.** Check the list against the unrecoverable set in step 2, item by item. This is where the finding usually is, and it is invisible if you only read what is written down. Missing spares of a prescribed or fitted item, missing documents, a missing charger for something on the list, and the item implied by an activity block that nobody wrote down. Run it first, before the cuts, so that a list already at its limit gets the addition costed against a cut rather than tacked on the end.
2. **The consumables pass.** Anything used up daily scales with trip length even though clothing does not, and it is the one place where the day count is the right number. Medication, contact lenses, a specific formulation, anything with a dose. On a long trip these are a genuine volume problem rather than a line item, and the fix is usually a different format of the same thing rather than less of it. Ask whether the destination stocks it, and note that "available in the capital" is not the same as available on the trip.
3. **The cut pass**, in the order given in step 6.

Say which items are volume-dominant before trimming anything small. Footwear, outerwear, and anything rigid set the size of a bag, because they do not compress and they cannot be redistributed. Cutting one pair of shoes is worth more than cutting five soft items, so work top-down by volume rather than down the list in order. Where the user's own list contains four of something rigid, that is the finding, and the small stuff barely matters until it is fixed.

## Step 6: Deliver

Give back:

1. The derived constraints in two lines: bag, weather range, laundry cycle, activity blocks.
2. The list, grouped by activity block and by the replaceable split, with numbers rather than "enough".
3. The cross-block items called out, since they are what makes the bag work.
4. The single-use heavy items as an explicit decision list, with the cost of each and what happens if it stays home.
5. The cabin bag contents, if anything is being checked.
6. What was assumed, and what to check against an official source.

Close with the net effect, in one short paragraph: what came out, what went in, and where that leaves them. A user who asked what to cut is asking whether it fits now, and a list of edits does not answer that.

If the user is over their limit, cut in this order: single-use heavy items first, then anything replaceable at the destination, then clothing volume by shortening the wash cycle, and only then the things that make the trip worse. Say which cut you are making and what it costs.

Then be honest about what you cannot know. You cannot tell from a list whether a bag will close or come in under a weight limit, and asserting that it now fits is a guess dressed as a verdict. Say so, and hand over the physical check instead: pack the actual bag several days out rather than the night before, and where there is a weight limit, put it on scales, because the limit is enforced in kilos and no list has ever been weighed. Several days out is the point of the exercise, since it leaves time to do something about the answer.

And say what happens if the cuts are not enough, because sometimes the constraint is simply not satisfiable and continuing to trim is the wrong response. At that point the constraint itself has to move, and the options are worth naming so the user can pick: a larger or second bag, checking something, shipping ahead or posting home, buying or renting at the destination, or dropping an activity block and its whole kit group. Dropping the block is usually the largest single saving available and it is the one nobody proposes. Which of those is acceptable is the user's call, not yours; the job is to say plainly that trimming has run out.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Producing a generic list for the destination and season, which is long, plausible, and misses both of the items that would actually have ruined the trip.
- One outfit per day, so a fortnight is packed as fourteen days of clothes when laundry on day five was available.
- Treating the seasonal average as the weather, and missing the overnight low, the rain, or the air conditioning.
- Packing hard around toiletries that are on sale two streets from the hotel, while the one irreplaceable cable gets a single line.
- Missing the internal flight with a smaller allowance than the one the bag was packed for.
- Carrying bulky equipment that is rented at the destination as a matter of course, without ever raising it as a choice.
- Filling the bag to its limit on the way out, with a return leg that has things to bring back.
- Putting medication, documents, or glasses in a checked bag.
- Reviewing a list by only subtracting from it, so the missing spare of a prescribed item survives the review untouched.
- Trimming soft items one at a time while four rigid, incompressible ones set the size of the bag.
- Treating a daily consumable as a single line item on a long trip, when its volume scales with every day of it.
- Declaring that a list now fits a bag or a weight limit, which cannot be known from a list and is a guess wearing a verdict's clothes.
- Trimming past the point where the constraint is satisfiable, instead of saying the constraint has to move.
- Treating children as a section at the end of a trip they are the whole shape of.
- Asserting a visa, medication import, or customs rule rather than pointing at where to check it.
