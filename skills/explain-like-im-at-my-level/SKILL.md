# Explain Like I'm At My Level

Finds where the person actually is, then explains from there. The two failure modes are symmetrical and both are common: an explanation pitched below them, which is patronising and tells them nothing, and one pitched above them, which uses a term they do not have and loses them in the first sentence while sounding authoritative.

The default behaviour is worse than either. A generic explanation aimed at nobody in particular starts too far back, then moves too fast, then ends without ever addressing the specific thing that was confusing.

## Do not invent the specifics

This skill is frequently reached through a medical, legal, or financial question, because those are the settings where someone is handed terminology and nods. Calibrating an explanation well makes it more persuasive, which raises rather than lowers the cost of getting a detail wrong: a confidently pitched number in an unfamiliar domain will be believed, repeated, and sometimes acted on.

So separate two things and keep the line visible. Explaining what a term means, what a mechanism is, and how a category of thing generally works is the job, and doing it accurately is the whole value. Supplying a specific figure, threshold, dose, rate, deadline, or entitlement is not, unless it is genuinely known — and where a number does real work in the explanation, give it as an order of magnitude, say where it comes from, and say what would make it different for this particular case.

Never invent a component of a named scale, scheme, or rule to make an explanation tidy. Where the exact contents matter, say what kind of thing it contains, and make finding out the specifics one of the questions the person asks the professional in front of them. That is a better outcome than a complete-looking answer, because it puts the authoritative version in the room where a decision is actually made.

And keep the person's own question separable from the advice they were given. Explaining what was said is not the same as endorsing or second-guessing it, and the person is usually asking for the first.

## When they have already told you where they are

The common case for this skill, and it makes most of the calibration section unnecessary. Someone who says what they have read, what they already understand, and what specifically is not landing has done the work: take them at their word, say in half a sentence what you are therefore assuming, and go straight to the block. Re-establishing a level they have just stated wastes the part of their attention you most need.

Being told a level does not tell you the purpose, though, which is a separate axis and usually still needs settling.

## Do not open with a quiz

The instinct once you decide to calibrate is to ask a series of assessment questions. Do not. It reads as a test, it makes an adult feel examined, and someone who is already embarrassed about not understanding something will disengage or overstate what they know.

Calibrate from what is already in front of you first, which is usually more than enough:

- **The vocabulary they used.** This is the strongest signal available and it is free. Someone who uses a technical term correctly and in passing has the concept; someone who uses it in quotation marks, or slightly wrong, or as the thing they are asking about, does not. Note which terms they deployed as tools and which they treated as unfamiliar objects.
- **The shape of the question.** A question that already contains a correct mental model and is asking about an edge case comes from a different place than one asking what something is for. "Why does X do Y when Z" is nearly an expert question whatever the vocabulary.
- **What they say they have already tried.** Someone who has read three articles does not need a fourth summary. They need the specific thing those articles all assumed, which is what is actually blocking them.
- **The domain they came from.** A person who is expert in something else is not a beginner in general, and the fastest route is usually an analogy to their own field, done carefully.
- **Why they want to know**, which sets the depth far more than their level does.

Where you genuinely cannot tell and it matters, ask **one** question, and make it a specific one that discriminates rather than a general one about their level. "How familiar are you with X" reliably produces a useless answer, because people answer it socially. A question about one concrete thing — whether they have used a particular tool, whether a specific term means anything to them, what they think happens in a particular case — gets a real answer and does the work of five general ones.

## Ask what the explanation is for, because it sets the depth

Level and purpose are different axes and the second matters more. The same person needs completely different explanations depending on what they are about to do:

- **A decision.** Shallowest. They need what changes their choice and nothing else: the options, the trade-off, and the consequence of each. Mechanism is a detour, and an explanation of how it works underneath is the commonest way this purpose gets answered wrongly.
- **Doing the thing themselves.** Depth exactly as far as the procedure goes, plus disproportionate attention to the specific places it goes wrong, which is what separates a usable explanation from a correct one.
- **Talking to someone about it.** Medium depth, but a different shape: the deliverable is largely not prose. It is the vocabulary, the questions worth asking, and enough model to tell a real answer from a vague one. The output is closer to a kit than an essay, and organising it as one is the single biggest improvement available for this purpose.
- **Explaining it onward to a child.** A particular case of the one below and worth treating separately, because the accuracy constraint tightens rather than loosens. A child who is asking follow-up questions is doing the thing you want and will test whatever you give them, so a convenient falsehood does not survive the week and costs the parent their credibility. Pitch the vocabulary down and the honesty up: keep the technical words out, keep the mechanism true, and say which part is a simplification and in which direction. Supply the sayable version, the picture and where it stops working, the two or three follow-ups that are actually coming, and one common wrong answer to avoid repeating. And say that admitting to not knowing and looking it up together is a legitimate move rather than a failure, because it is the one that teaches most.
- **Explaining it onward.** One level deeper than they will deliver, because teaching costs more than knowing. Distinct from the previous case in direction: there they are receiving answers and need to interrogate them, here they are giving them and need to survive questions. So hand over the one- or two-sentence version they can actually say, the reusable analogy, where it breaks, and the two or three follow-ups they should be ready for.
- **Satisfying curiosity.** Deepest, and the one case where the mechanism is the point rather than the scaffolding.

Ask, or infer and say what you inferred. A perfectly pitched explanation of the wrong aspect is still a failed explanation, and this is the more common miss.

## Find the actual blocking point

When someone says they still do not understand after several attempts, the problem is almost never the level. It is one specific unstated assumption sitting underneath every explanation they have read, invisible to the people writing them because everyone in the field acquired it so early they forgot it was learned.

So look for the missing prerequisite rather than restating more simply. Restating more simply is what has already failed. The usual shapes:

- **A word used in a domain sense that also has an everyday sense**, which is the single commonest cause of persistent confusion. The reader maps it to the everyday meaning and everything afterwards is subtly wrong in a way they cannot locate. Say the term, say what it means here, and say explicitly what it does not mean.
- **A step everyone skips as obvious**, usually about what is actually happening physically, or who is doing what to what.
- **A missing motivation.** They understand what it does and have never been told what problem it exists to solve, so nothing hangs together and none of it stays.
- **A false model that fits most of the evidence.** They have a working theory that is wrong, and new information gets absorbed into it rather than replacing it. This is the hardest one, because more explanation makes it worse, and it needs the wrong model surfaced and contradicted directly.

Where they have described their current understanding, read it for the false model specifically. Where they have not, the fastest move is often to ask what they think happens, or to state the wrong model most people hold and check whether it is theirs. Being handed the misconception you actually hold, named out loud, is a much better experience than being talked at for another page.

## Pitch it

Then explain, with these constraints:

- **Start at their edge, not at the beginning.** The thing they already know is the foundation, and rebuilding it costs their attention and their patience. Say what you are assuming they have, in a half-sentence, so they can stop you.
- **Introduce at most a few new terms**, and define each one where it first appears rather than in a preamble. An explanation that front-loads definitions is a glossary, and nobody retains a glossary.
- **Use their words for things they already named.** Switching to the field's preferred term for a thing they just described in their own words makes the whole explanation feel like a correction.
- **One idea per paragraph, and make the load visible.** Say when something is genuinely hard, and say when something is simpler than it sounds. Both are reassuring in different ways, and the second is often the most useful sentence in an explanation, because a lot of confusion is people assuming there must be more to it.
- **Concrete before abstract, always.** A worked case, then the general rule. The reverse order is how textbooks are written and it is why people bounce off them.
- **Do not simplify into something false.** This is the boundary of the whole skill. A simplification that will have to be unlearned is worse than an honest statement that the full answer is more complicated, and the honest version can be delivered in a clause: say the simple version, then flag in a few words that it is a simplification and in which direction it is wrong. That clause costs almost nothing and it is the difference between an explanation someone can build on and one they will have to demolish.

## Length

Every constraint above pushes toward more words, and none of them pushes back. Left alone this produces a long, careful, correct explanation that is worse than a short one, because attention is the resource actually being spent.

The governing rule is that length is set by the purpose, not by the subject's complexity or by the person's level. A decision needs a few hundred words however deep the topic goes. Something they have to do themselves runs as long as the steps and their failure points. Curiosity is the only purpose that licenses real length.

The working test on each paragraph: does this change what they do, ask, or decide? If not, it is there for completeness, and completeness is not what was asked for. Depth they did not ask for is offered at the end as a direction rather than delivered in the middle.

## Analogies

An analogy is the most effective and most dangerous tool here.

Take it from something they have demonstrated they know, which is why the domain they came from matters. An analogy to something they do not know is two explanations.

And say where it breaks, every time and in the same breath. An unbounded analogy gets extended by the listener into exactly the region where it is wrong, and they will then reason confidently from a false conclusion, which is worse than the confusion they started with. The break is not a footnote; it is half the content.

Where an analogy is doing so much work that its limits would take longer than the real explanation, drop it and explain the thing directly.

Use few. Each one costs the reader a context switch and a set of limits to hold, so a piece carrying several of them is asking them to track several parallel worlds at once. One that does most of the work is worth more than three that each do a little.

## Check, without making it a test

Do not end by asking whether that made sense. It reliably gets yes, from politeness, and it puts the burden of assessment on the person least able to do it, since not knowing what you have not understood is the definition of the situation.

Better closes:

- Name the next thing that usually trips people up, which invites a question without demanding one.
- Offer the two directions explicitly: further down into the mechanism, or outward into what to do with it.
- Where they need to explain it onward, hand them the version they can say in one or two sentences, and separately the thing to be ready for if they are asked.
- Ask them to say back the part that matters most for their purpose, framed as checking your own explanation rather than checking them. That framing is not a courtesy; it changes who is being assessed and it gets a real answer.

## When they are an expert in something adjacent

Common and easy to get wrong in both directions.

They will move much faster than a beginner and they will have specific, load-bearing misconceptions imported from their own field, where a shared word means something different. Find the false friend early, because everything downstream depends on it.

Do not explain their own field back to them, and do not over-correct into pitching above them either. The register that works is peer to peer with the unfamiliar parts made explicit: assume the general competence, assume none of the specific content.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Opening with an assessment quiz, which makes an adult feel examined.
- Asking how familiar someone is with a subject, which gets a socially calibrated answer rather than a true one.
- Explaining at a level when the actual variable was purpose, so the depth is right and the aspect is wrong.
- Restating the same explanation more simply when the block is a missing prerequisite, which no amount of simplification supplies.
- Starting from the beginning for someone who told you where they already are.
- Front-loading definitions.
- Simplifying into something the person will later have to unlearn.
- An analogy with no stated limit, which the listener extends into the region where it is false.
- Using the field's term for the thing they just described in their own words, which reads as a correction.
- Recalibrating a level the person has already stated, which spends the attention you needed for the explanation itself.
- Letting the length be set by the subject rather than by the purpose, so a decision gets a thousand words of mechanism.
- Stacking analogies, so the reader tracks three parallel worlds and their three sets of limits.
- Inventing a figure, threshold, or the components of a named scale to make an explanation feel complete, in exactly the domains where being believed matters most.
- Handing a child a simplification that is false, when they are asking follow-ups and will find it.
- Asking whether that made sense, which gets a yes.
