# Book Notes Synthesizer

Produces the few ideas from a book that are worth carrying, with what each one costs the reader in changed belief or changed behaviour attached. The output is deliberately small: a book yields two or three things worth keeping, occasionally five, and a write-up that returns twenty has returned the table of contents.

It is not a summary and does not substitute for reading. Where someone wants to know what is in a book they have not read, that is a different request and should be answered as one, plainly, rather than dressed as synthesis.

**Two forks before the procedure.** If the book makes no argument — a novel, a memoir, most poetry — go to "Fiction, memoir, and anything not making an argument" now, because the steps in between are written for books with claims and will mislead you into extracting a lesson that is not there. If the request covers several books, several readers, or both, read "Many books, or many readers" as well.

## The test that decides everything

An idea is worth keeping if at least one of these is true:

- **It changed what the reader believes.** Something they thought was true and now do not, or the reverse.
- **It changes what they will do**, specifically enough to be checkable.
- **It gives a name to something they already knew but could not point at**, which is worth more than it sounds, because a named pattern becomes usable and available in argument.
- **It is a fact or a mechanism they will need and cannot easily reconstruct.**

An idea that is merely interesting, agreeable, or well put does not qualify. The most common failure here is keeping the sentences that were pleasing to read, which is why highlights are a poor guide to value: people highlight fluency.

Apply the test out loud and say what did not pass. A list of five kept ideas plus a line on what was discarded is more useful than a list of fifteen, and the discarding is most of the work.

## Step 1: Work out what you actually have

The material arrives in very different states and it changes what is possible.

- **Highlights or a Kindle export.** Common, and systematically biased. Highlights over-represent the beginning of the book, aphorisms, and anything quotable, and under-represent arguments that took ten pages to build — which are usually the book's real contribution. Say this, and read the highlights for where the argument was rather than only for what was marked.
- **Margin notes and marginalia.** Much better signal, because a note is a reaction rather than a recognition. The disagreements especially.
- **Nothing but memory.** Fine, and often the best filter available: what survived unaided several weeks after finishing is a strong indicator of what mattered. Work from what they remember, and say that the forgetting has already done a pass for you.
- **The book itself, unread by the user.** Not this skill. Say so.

Thin notes are the normal case, not the exception, and the response to them is not to refuse. Two different things get confused here and the distinction is what makes the output honest.

**Do not substitute the book's argument for the reader's engagement with it.** Reconstructing what the book says and presenting it as their synthesis is writing a summary and calling it notes. That is the thing being avoided.

**Do bring what you know about the book's standing, and mark it as yours.** Whether the evidence has held up, what has been contested since, which sections carry the argument, what the strongest counter-position is — none of that is available from anyone's highlights, and withholding it to stay pure produces a worse answer. Label it clearly as your contribution rather than theirs or the book's, so the reader can tell which parts they can claim as their own reading.

Where the sample of notes is partial, say what fraction you are working from, and say what a fuller set would most likely change.

## Step 1b: When the reader has not said what they remember

Common, and not an obstacle. Someone who says a book stayed with them but cannot say why is describing a real state, and asking them to articulate why is asking for the hard version of exactly what they came for help with.

Ask oblique questions instead, which get answers where the direct one does not. What comes back unprompted rather than what they would nominate as important. Which part they found themselves arguing with. What they expected before starting and where that was wrong. What they have already repeated to someone else, which is the strongest single indicator of what landed. Where they stopped reading, or wanted to.

The disagreement question matters most and should always be among them, for the reason in step 3: it is the most valuable material and the fastest to fade. And a blank answer to it is itself a finding.

Where no answer is possible because there is only one exchange, say what you would have asked and answer with what you can, marked as yours.

## Step 2: Find the actual argument, not the topic

Most books have one claim and a great deal of support. Name the claim in a sentence, in the strongest and most specific form the book actually supports.

Then check it is a claim rather than a topic. "The book is about attention" is a topic. "The book argues that attention is a resource that markets can extract, and that most of what looks like personal failure is that extraction working" is a claim, and only a claim can be agreed or disagreed with.

Separate the claim from the machinery around it. A book usually has:

- **The central claim.** Often smaller than the book, and often the thing that would have made a strong essay.
- **The framework or vocabulary**, which is sometimes the most useful part and sometimes a repackaging of something ordinary in proprietary terms. Say which, because a book's coined terms are the most quotable and least durable part of it.
- **The evidence**, whose quality matters and is worth commenting on.
- **The examples**, which are memorable and often carry more weight in the reader's memory than the argument they were supposed to illustrate.

Where the book's title or reputation says something different from what the book actually argues, note the gap. It is common and it is worth knowing.

## Step 3: Connect it to what the reader already believes

This is the step that turns notes into something retained, and it is what every generic summary skips.

For each candidate idea, ask which of these it is:

- **Confirming.** They already thought this and now have a better articulation, a name, or evidence. Useful, and worth marking as confirming rather than new, because confirming ideas feel like insight and are the ones most likely to be over-credited.
- **Contradicting.** It cuts against something they believe. This is the most valuable category by a distance and the one that fades fastest, because the reader's existing model quietly reabsorbs it within weeks. Write these down explicitly as a conflict: what they thought, what the book claims, and what would settle it.
- **Connecting.** It links to something from another book, their work, or an argument they have been having. Note the link, since a connected idea is retained and an isolated one is not.
- **New territory.** Genuinely unfamiliar. Rarer than it feels.

Push for the contradictions specifically, because they are the reason to read anything. If a reader reports that a book confirmed everything they already thought, that is a finding: either they are not reading it adversarially, or the book was chosen to agree with them, and either is worth saying.

## Step 4: Read it against the reader, not in the abstract

Ask what they are actually working on, deciding, or arguing about. The same book yields different keepers for different readers, and a synthesis written for nobody is a summary again.

Then be specific about scope. Most non-fiction arguments are true within a range and are sold as universal, so name where the claim applies and where it does not, particularly if the reader is about to apply it somewhere the book's evidence never reached. A management idea drawn from large companies does not automatically hold for a team of four, and saying so is more valuable than the idea.

## Step 5: Be honest about the book

Say what did not hold up. This is not a review, but a synthesis that presents everything as equally sound is not usable.

Worth flagging:

- **Evidence that is thinner than the confidence.** Anecdotes doing the work of data, a striking study carrying a whole chapter, results from a narrow population generalised widely.
- **The unfalsifiable claim**, which explains every outcome and predicts none.
- **Where the book is out of date**, especially anything empirical.
- **The one-idea book**, meaning a good article extended to book length. Say so, and say which chapters carry it, which is genuinely useful to someone deciding whether to reread.
- **Where the reader's own notes show them agreeing too easily.** A run of highlights with no arguments back usually means the book was congenial rather than right.

Do not fabricate criticism for balance. If it is a good book, say so.

## Step 6: Give it somewhere to go

Notes that are filed are forgotten. Two things prevent it and both are small.

**A retrieval hook.** Not a summary of the book but the sentence that will bring it back: the claim in the reader's own words, the one example that carries it, and the situation in which it applies. The situation is the part that matters, since an idea is retrieved by circumstance rather than by title.

**One thing to actually do**, where the book warrants it. Small, dated, and checkable. Not "think differently about X". Where the book does not warrant an action, say so rather than inventing one, because an invented action is the fastest way to make the whole write-up feel like a productivity exercise and get abandoned.

Then, briefly: what to read next if they want the argument challenged rather than repeated, and whether the book is worth rereading or whether the notes now replace it.

## Many books, or many readers

A year of reading reviewed at once, a reading group, a team, a course. The single-book procedure still applies to each item, but three things change and they are what make the difference between a useful session and nine recaps.

**Do not rescue the forgotten ones.** The books nobody can remember are the most interesting result available, and spending the effort reminding everyone what they were converts the finding into homework. Ask why instead: a bad book, a bad month, or a book so congenial that nothing caught.

**Rate again, cold, and use the difference.** A score recorded on the night measures how the evening went. A score given months later from memory measures what survived. The gap between them is the actual output: a book that dropped was pleasant and hollow, and one that climbed kept arguing after everyone had finished. This works for a single reader reviewing their own year just as well as for a group.

**Budget keepers across the whole set, not per book.** Two to five per book multiplied by nine books is a reading list, not a synthesis. A year yields a handful of things that hold, and the second pass — cutting across books rather than within them — is where the value is. Ideas that appeared in more than one book, and the places two books contradicted each other, are the strongest candidates.

**With a group, add the disagreements between people** to the disagreements with books. An unresolved argument between two members is the same category as a contradiction and is worth more than anything the room agreed about. Ask for it directly, because it will not be volunteered.

## Step 7: Deliver

Keep it short. The format that works:

1. **The claim**, in one sentence.
2. **The two to five keepers**, each with its category from step 3 and, for the contradictions, what it argues with.
3. **The retrieval hooks**, one line each.
4. **What did not survive the test**, in a line or two.
5. **Where it does not hold**, including the reader's own intended application.
6. **The one action**, if there is one.
7. **What is yours rather than the reader's or the book's**, marked.

Where the notes only support two keepers, deliver two. Padding to a target is the failure this skill exists to avoid.

## Fiction, memoir, and anything not making an argument

The framework above assumes a book with a claim. Much reading has none, and forcing an argument out of a novel produces the worst kind of book note.

Do not convert a novel into lessons. A reader who wanted lessons would not have read a novel. But the sections above then leave nothing in their place, so here is what replaces them.

**The keeper test changes.** An element is worth keeping if it recurs unbidden, if it has changed how the reader sees a real person or situation, if it gave them a feeling they can locate but had not named, or if they argued with it. Note that four of those are about the reader rather than about the book, which is why a synthesis of fiction cannot be written without them and why step 1b matters more here than anywhere else.

**The equivalent of the claim is the gap between the book's reputation and the reading.** Most well-known novels are carried around as a one-line summary that turns out to describe a small fraction of the experience of reading them. Naming that gap is usually the most useful thing available, because it is what the reader half-noticed and could not articulate.

**The retrieval hook is a scene or a sentence, not a proposition.** The situation that brings it back is a human one rather than a decision: the kind of person, the kind of moment.

**There is often no action, and that is fine.** Where the reader has a purpose — and with fiction the purpose is frequently a conversation with a particular person rather than anything they intend to do — read the book against that person specifically. What they will already have, what they will not, and what only this reader can bring. A good question to ask someone else is a better output than a summary, and it is usually what was actually wanted.

The honest note may still be that the book was worth reading and yields nothing extractable. That is a legitimate result and should be said plainly rather than worked around.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Producing a chapter summary, which is what the request usually looks like and never what it means.
- Returning fifteen ideas, which is a table of contents wearing a synthesis costume.
- Treating highlights as a record of what mattered, when they are a record of what was quotable.
- Missing the argument that took ten pages to build because nothing in it was highlightable.
- Naming the topic instead of the claim, so nothing in the notes can be agreed or disagreed with.
- Losing the contradictions, which are the reason to have read the book and the first things to fade.
- Writing for a generic reader, when the same book yields different keepers depending on what someone is doing.
- Presenting a claim as universal when the evidence came from one narrow setting.
- Inventing an action item for a book that does not imply one.
- Extracting lessons from fiction.
- Filling in what the book says from general knowledge when the notes are thin, without marking it as yours.
- Withholding what you know about a book's standing in order to stay pure, when the reader cannot get that from their own highlights and it is the thing that changes how much to trust their notes.
- Asking a reader to articulate why a book stayed with them, which is the hard question they came for help with, instead of the oblique ones that actually produce answers.
- Handling fiction with a prohibition and no method, so a novel gets either a lesson it does not contain or nothing at all.
- Reviewing a year or a group by producing one recap per book, and rescuing the forgotten ones instead of treating the forgetting as the result.
- Trusting a score given on the night, which records how the evening went rather than what lasted.
