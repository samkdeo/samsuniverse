# Spaced Repetition Deck Builder

Builds a review system: what goes in, how each item is written, what the daily load will actually be, and what to do when a card stops working. The cards are the part people ask for and the smallest part of the answer, because a deck of bad cards costs more than no deck at all — it consumes review time daily and returns knowledge that only works in the presence of the card.

It is not a summariser and not a note-taking system. Spaced repetition is for retrieving specific things from memory on demand. Most material handed to it is not that, and saying so is the most useful thing this skill does.

## Step 1: Decide what should not be a card

Do this first and do it out loud, because the instinct is to convert everything, and conversion is the expensive mistake: every card is a permanent daily obligation, so the deck should contain only what is worth paying for forever.

**Good candidates**, meaning things needed from memory, on demand, without a prompt available:

- Vocabulary and terminology, in either direction
- Facts with a stable answer: dates, values, classifications, names, thresholds
- Formulas and their conditions of use
- Anything where the retrieval speed itself matters, such as a clinical, legal, or performance context where looking it up is not available
- Correspondences: this maps to that

**Bad candidates**, which should be handled another way and said so:

- **Anything not yet understood.** This is the big one and it is not negotiable. Memorising an explanation you cannot follow produces a card you will fail forever, and every failure feels like a memory problem when it is a comprehension problem. Understand first, then card it. When material arrives that the user clearly has not digested, say so and route them to learning it before scheduling it.
- **Skills and procedures.** Playing, writing, coding, speaking, diagnosing, operating. These need practice, not recall. A card can hold a fact the skill uses; it cannot hold the skill.
- **Things only ever needed on recognition**, where seeing the thing in context is enough and unprompted recall is never required.
- **Anything reliably to hand.** If it is looked up in seconds whenever needed and speed does not matter, the card is a tax with no benefit.
- **Judgement, argument, and anything with a contested answer.** A card enforces one answer, so it teaches the user to be confident about something that is not settled.
- **Whole definitions, paragraphs, and lists as written.** These need breaking up, which is step 3, or they need to stay in notes.

Say what you excluded and why. A user who asked for a deck of everything and received a smaller deck plus reasons has been given the more valuable thing.

## Step 2: Establish the deadline, because it changes the method

Ask whether there is a fixed date, and treat the two cases separately, since much of the standard advice is written for the open-ended case and misfires against an exam.

**No deadline**, meaning permanent retention of a language, a professional vocabulary, a body of knowledge. Long intervals are the point, the daily load should be small and indefinitely sustainable, and there is no rush to introduce everything.

**A fixed date.** Now the calendar constrains the algorithm rather than the reverse. Cards introduced very close to the date will not have had a real spaced review and are effectively being crammed, which works for a few days and does not stick. So work backwards: get everything introduced early enough that each item has had several spaced exposures before the date, and stop introducing new cards some way out, because a deck still growing in the final stretch produces a review load exactly when revision time is most contested.

If the honest arithmetic says the material cannot be introduced and consolidated in the time available, say so and cut rather than compressing. A schedule that cannot be executed is not a plan.

Cutting needs a method, because "prioritise" is not one when the material exceeds capacity several times over. Work capacity first: the sustainable daily rate multiplied by the days available before introduction stops. That number is the deck's size, and everything else is selection against it. Then select by three tests, in order. **What is actually assessed**, in the form it is assessed in, since a deck built on a syllabus rather than on the assessment wastes a share of a fixed budget. **What cannot be looked up in the moment that matters**, which is the whole reason for recall. And **what is load-bearing for the rest**, meaning items other knowledge hangs off, which pay for themselves twice.

Say the ratio out loud. A user who learns they can carry perhaps a fifth of the material has been given the real problem, and choosing which fifth deliberately beats discovering in the final week that the early chapters were carded exhaustively and the later ones never reached.

**After the date matters too.** Say plainly whether the material is wanted afterwards. A deck built for an exam and then abandoned is fine and should be planned for. A deck built for an exam in a subject the user will practise professionally should be maintained, and knowing which changes what goes in it.

## Material that expires, and recurring occasions

A third case that neither of the above covers, and which needs a different architecture rather than a different schedule: material needed intensely for one occasion, never again afterwards, with a new set arriving on a repeating cycle. Names and details before an event, a caseload for a shift, a class list each year, an account before a meeting.

Spaced repetition is the wrong shape for this by default and can be actively harmful, so say so before building anything.

**Split the material in two.** There is a reusable spine — the parts that are the same every time and will be used for years — and there is the per-occasion set, which is disposable. Only the spine belongs in a permanent deck, and building it once makes every future occasion cheaper. Users almost never separate these themselves, and the separation is the main insight to hand them.

**The per-occasion set is a rehearsal, not a deck.** Build it days rather than months ahead, run several spaced retrievals working backwards from the date, and delete or suspend the whole set immediately afterwards. The deletion is not tidiness, it is the load-bearing step.

**Because the real danger is interference between sets.** Successive occasions produce near-identical questions with different answers, which is the exact configuration that yields confident wrong answers rather than forgotten ones. In most contexts a wrong answer is a nuisance; in a live occasion in front of the people concerned it is the catastrophic failure. Two rules contain it: every card in a per-occasion set carries the identifying name on the front so the sets cannot fuse, and two sets are never rehearsed in the same sitting.

**A short fixed ladder is correct here**, and this is the one place it is. There are too few repetitions for performance-driven intervals to have anything to work with, and the schedule is set by the date rather than by recall. Say that this is deliberately not spaced repetition, so the user does not carry the ladder back into their permanent deck.

**Watch for the material that is not a memory problem at all.** Where the complaint is about looking as though one is reading rather than about forgetting, the honest answer often involves the format of what is held rather than the contents of memory: which parts must be known cold because looking down at them is fatal, and which can stay on paper because consulting them is unremarkable. Say which is which rather than carding everything.

## Step 3: Write cards that will survive being asked in four months

Card quality is the whole game and it is where a generated deck usually fails, because bad cards are invisible on the day they are written and obvious in March.

**One item per card.** The commonest defect is a card carrying several facts, which cannot be graded: the user gets two of three and has to decide what that means, and whatever they decide is wrong. Split it.

**The question must be answerable from itself.** A card divorced from its source becomes unanswerable, and "What is the third condition?" is meaningless months later. Every card carries enough context to identify what is being asked without being so wordy that it gives away the answer.

**One correct answer, and the user should know when they have it.** If two reasonable answers exist, the question is underspecified. Narrow it.

**Ask for the thing that is actually needed, in the direction it is needed.** Recognising a term and producing it are different abilities and each needs its own card, so decide which the user needs rather than making both by default: making both doubles the load for a benefit only sometimes wanted.

**Prefer cloze deletions for anything embedded in a sentence**, since they keep the context and are much faster to write. Delete the load-bearing word, not an incidental one, and make sure what remains does not give the answer away.

**Lists are the classic failure and need converting rather than carding.** A card asking for all seven of something will be failed on six correct answers forever. Convert it: one card per item asking what that item does; a card that asks how many; cards that ask which one covers a given case. Where the order genuinely matters, overlapping clozes that each hide one element work far better than a single recall of the whole sequence.

**Make it concrete and personal where you can.** A card connected to a real example, a real case, or something the user already knows is remembered far better than an abstract phrasing, and the effect is large enough to be worth the extra seconds.

**Avoid interference.** Cards that are near-identical in wording but differ in answer will be confused with each other permanently. When two items are similar, write the cards to emphasise what distinguishes them rather than what they share.

**Do not put anything on a card that the user has not verified.** Where the material came from the user, quote it. Where you generated it, say so, and say it needs checking before it is studied, because a card is a machine for installing something in memory and installing an error is worse than not knowing.

## Step 4: Tell the user what the daily load will actually be

The most-skipped step and the reason most decks are abandoned.

The arithmetic that matters is not the size of the deck. Every card comes due again on a repeating cycle, so the steady daily review count is roughly the number of cards divided by their average interval in days. That relationship is the thing to give the user, because it is what makes the cost visible: a deck of a given size at a given maturity implies a daily load whether anyone planned for it or not, and doubling the deck doubles the load forever.

Two consequences fall straight out of it and are worth saying. Cards get cheaper as their intervals lengthen, so a mature deck costs far less per card than a new one, which is why deleting a mature deck is expensive and why the early weeks of a new one feel disproportionately hard. And the load is set by the rate cards are added at, not by the total, since the total is just the rate multiplied by how long it has been running.

**Measure rather than estimate wherever you can.** Any real tool reports the projected daily load directly, and its number is built from this user's actual performance rather than from assumed averages. Send them to it. Where you must estimate instead, use the relationship above, show the working, and say plainly that it is a model rather than a measurement — do not present invented per-card timings as though they were known. The honest version is a range with its assumptions visible, checked against reality after a week.

So make it explicit before they start. Give the number of new cards a day, the expected review time as it settles rather than in week one, and the point at which it will stop growing. Someone who understands they are signing up for a daily commitment will choose a rate they can keep, and the smaller rate maintained is worth far more than the larger one abandoned.

Two consequences worth stating:

- **Introduce new cards at a rate the user can sustain on their worst day, not their best.** The failure is a skipped week producing a backlog that is itself the reason for the next skipped week.
- **A backlog is fixed by reducing the new-card rate to zero until it clears, not by grinding through it.** Say this in advance, because it is the moment most decks die.

Where the user already has a deck they are drowning in, this is usually the whole diagnosis, and the fix is to stop adding, clear the backlog, and then prune.

## Rescuing a deck that has stopped working

Named in the description and the harder half of the job: someone with a large deck, a frightening backlog, and cards they keep failing. Do not start building. Work in this order, because doing it in any other order wastes effort.

**First, talk them out of deleting it.** This is almost always the proposal and it is usually wrong, though not always, so check which case this is before arguing. Deletion is genuinely right for a small deck of cards that were badly written from the start, for a shared deck the user did not write and does not agree with, and for material they have decided not to pursue. What makes deletion expensive is maturity, not size: it is the accumulated intervals being thrown away, not the card count. Where the deck is large and has been running for months, the case below holds. A large deck's mature cards are the cheap ones — long intervals, seconds each — and they represent the entire return on months of work. Deleting them to fix a problem caused by a small number of bad cards and one setting means paying full price to relearn what they already know. Say this plainly and first.

**Second, separate the three problems**, which arrive fused and have different fixes: a backlog, caused by an addition rate that was survivable on good weeks; a small set of repeatedly failed cards eating a disproportionate share of the time; and possibly a deck simply too large for the sessions they will do, which is invisible until the first two clear.

**Third, stop the inflow.** New cards to zero, before anything else, and staying at zero until the backlog is empty. Grinding a backlog while still adding is what produces the next skipped week.

**Fourth, take the fear out of the backlog with arithmetic.** A large due count is mostly mature cards answered in seconds, not that many unknown items. Have them time a small batch and multiply, so the number becomes a couple of unpleasant evenings rather than an unbounded dread. Where it is still too big to face, cap the daily reviews and let it drain over a week or two rather than resetting anything: the existing intervals are hard-won information and discarding them is the same mistake as deleting the deck.

**Fifth, find the failing cards by query rather than by memory**, using whatever the tool offers for lapse counts, and tune the threshold until the list is small enough to fix in an hour. Then apply the reformulation rules in "Handle the cards that keep failing" to each. This is the step that makes reviews stop feeling like punishment, and it is usually an hour's work.

**Sixth, check whether both directions are being tested.** On an existing deck this is frequently half the load, and it is invisible because it was set once at the start. Ask which direction the user actually needs. Suspending the direction they do not need can cut the daily load substantially for a loss they may not care about, and it is reversible.

**Only then** turn new cards back on, at a rate set by their worst week rather than their best. The old rate is disproved by the situation they are in.

## Step 5: The schedule

If the user has a spaced repetition tool, the tool sets the intervals and hand-writing a schedule is worse than the algorithm. Say so, and spend the effort on the cards and the daily rate instead, which are the parts the tool cannot fix.

Where there is no tool and reviews are on paper or in a spreadsheet, give an expanding schedule and be clear about what governs it. The interval grows when an item is recalled and collapses when it is not, so the schedule is driven by performance rather than by the calendar. A fixed ladder applied regardless of whether the user actually remembered is not spaced repetition, it is a rota. A workable manual version is a small number of boxes, each reviewed at a longer interval, where a correct answer promotes the card and a failure returns it to the first box.

Whatever the mechanism, three rules hold:

- **Review is retrieval, not rereading.** The user must produce the answer before seeing it. Rereading feels productive, is much easier, and is close to worthless for retention, which is why people prefer it.
- **Grade honestly.** A card recalled after a long struggle is not a success and marking it as one hides the item until it has been forgotten completely.
- **Short daily sessions beat long infrequent ones**, and consistency matters more than duration.

## Step 6: Handle the cards that keep failing

Every deck develops a small number of items that are failed repeatedly, and they consume a share of review time out of all proportion to their number.

The rule is to reformulate rather than to grind. Repeated failure is almost always a property of the card, not of the user's memory, and the usual causes are specific: the card carries more than one item, it lacks the context needed to answer, it interferes with a similar card, or the underlying material was never understood. Diagnose which, and rewrite accordingly — usually by splitting it, adding context, or going back and learning the thing properly.

And be willing to delete. A card failed many times that is not important enough to justify rewriting should be removed. The deck exists to serve the user and a card that has been failed a dozen times is charging rent.

## Step 7: Deliver

Give back:

1. What was excluded and why, with what to do with it instead.
2. The cards, in a format that can be imported, with the separator stated.
3. The new-card rate, the expected settled daily load, and the date the deck stops growing.
4. Anything you generated rather than took from the source, marked for checking.
5. The first thing to do if it starts slipping, decided now.

Where the deck is large, hand over a small sample first and ask whether the card style is right before generating the rest. A hundred cards in the wrong format is worse than ten in the right one.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Converting everything supplied into cards, when the most useful output is a smaller deck and a list of what does not belong in one.
- Carding material the user does not yet understand, producing items that will be failed forever for reasons memory cannot fix.
- Multi-fact cards that cannot be graded.
- Cards that cannot be answered without the source they came from.
- One card asking for a list of seven things, which is a permanent failure.
- Making both directions of every item by default, doubling the load for a benefit often not wanted.
- Near-identical cards with different answers, which interfere permanently.
- Presenting generated content as verified fact on a card, which installs errors on purpose.
- Never stating the daily review load, which is the reason most decks are abandoned.
- Introducing new cards right up to an exam, so the review load peaks exactly when it is least affordable.
- Hand-writing a schedule for a user whose software already does it better.
- Grinding a failing card instead of rewriting or deleting it.
- Inventing per-card timings and presenting the resulting daily load as a measurement.
- Answering a drowning user by building them more cards, or by agreeing that deleting a mature deck is the fix.
- Resetting the intervals on a backlog, which discards the most valuable thing in the deck.
- Saying "prioritise" to someone with five times more material than time, without a method for choosing.
- Building a permanent deck out of material with a one-occasion life, so successive sets interfere and produce confident wrong answers about the wrong person.
- Treating a problem of poise or format as a problem of memory, and carding what should have stayed on paper.
