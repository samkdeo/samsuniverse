# Contract Negotiation Prep

Prepares one negotiation: what the person will accept, what they will trade, in what order, and what they will actually say. It produces positions and sentences rather than a number, because a number with no position behind it collapses at the first push.

It is not legal advice. Where the contract contains terms with real financial or legal exposure, this skill will name them and say they are worth a lawyer's hour, and it will not tell the user whether a clause is enforceable in their jurisdiction. Say that once, plainly, and get on with the part that is actually being asked about.

## The escape hatch

Someone who asks a narrow question — should I take this, is this rate reasonable, how do I reply to this one email — gets a narrow answer. Run the full preparation for a real negotiation with something at stake: a large piece of work, a rate increase with an existing client, a contract with terms attached, or a conversation they are dreading.

## Step 1: Establish what happens if this does not go ahead

Everything else depends on this and most people skip it, then negotiate from a position they have not examined.

Ask what they will actually do if the deal falls through. Not what they would like to do. The honest answer is usually one of:

- **A real alternative.** Other work already available, a pipeline, a full schedule. This is a strong position and it should change how the negotiation is conducted, because someone who can genuinely walk negotiates differently and the other side can hear it.
- **Nothing lined up, and it matters.** Rent, a gap, a quiet quarter. This is common and it is not shameful, but it has to be stated out loud, because a negotiation run as though there were alternatives when there are none tends to end in either an accepted bad deal or a lost one, both of which are avoidable.
- **Nothing lined up, and it does not matter yet.** Runway, another income, a partner's salary. Effectively a strong position.

Then get the number that goes with it: the point below which the work is not worth doing. Build it from what the work actually costs them — the days, the overhead, the tax, the unpaid time, the work they would be turning down — rather than from what feels acceptable. A walk-away arrived at by feel drifts under pressure, and one arrived at by arithmetic does not.

**When you cannot ask, hand back the subtraction rather than skipping it.** The walk-away is load-bearing for everything downstream, so a plan built without it has to say so. Give the user the calculation to run in the specific terms of their situation — the days this actually takes including the unpaid stages nobody costed, minus tax and overhead, minus what they are turning down — and tell them to write the answer down before the conversation. Then mark which parts of the plan change depending on where it lands. A prep sheet with one honest gap in it is usable; one that quietly assumes a number is not.

**Do not supply market rates you do not have.** This is the most dangerous improvisation available in this skill, because a confident benchmark is exactly what a nervous user will repeat in the room, and being corrected on it costs them the whole position. What you can do safely is arithmetic on the numbers they gave you — the offer divided by the units, the gap against their own stated rate, what the terms are worth on top — which is usually more persuasive than any benchmark, because it is unarguable and it is about this deal. Where a real benchmark would help, say where it actually lives: their professional body or guild, published rate cards, and peers doing the same work, and note that going to find one is worth more than an hour of preparation.

Where the honest walk-away is very low, say so and change the strategy rather than pretending. A weak position is not a reason to accept anything; it is a reason to negotiate on the things that do not cost the client money, which is most of step 3.

## Step 2: Work out the other side's constraints

Most freelancers prepare only their own position, and the other side's constraints determine what is actually available.

Establish, or ask the user to find out:

- **Whether the person negotiating can say yes.** An awful lot of pushback comes from someone with no authority to agree, and the entire negotiation is with the wrong person. If they cannot approve it, the goal of the conversation changes: the job is to give them what they need to get it approved.
- **Where the budget comes from and when it resets.** A refusal is frequently about timing rather than value, and the same number that is impossible in one period is routine in the next. The deadline that matters is usually not the one on the contract: it is the date the other side's budget is fixed, which is often months earlier, and an ask that arrives after it is talking to someone who genuinely cannot say yes whatever they think of it. Find that date and work backwards from it. The same applies to any notice period buried in the existing agreement, which can move a renewal conversation much earlier than the renewal.
- **What their alternative is.** Whether they have other candidates, what it would cost them to start again, and how much of a hurry they are in. A deadline on their side is the single largest source of leverage a freelancer has, and it is often visible in the brief.
- **What they are actually buying.** Cheap and fine, or specific and right. A client buying a commodity will win on price and one buying judgement will not, and the same rate is a different conversation in each case.

Where the user does not know these, the highest-value action is often to go and find out before negotiating rather than to prepare a better argument.

## Step 3: Find the levers that are not the rate

The most useful move in a freelance negotiation is usually not on price, and a user fixated on the number is leaving most of the value on the table. Price is the one term the client's procurement is watching, and several of the others cost them nothing while being worth a great deal.

Go through these and mark which matter to this user, which are cheap to the client, and which are being quietly given away:

- **Scope, which is the big one.** Almost every rate objection is a scope conversation wearing a price costume, and the correct response to "that is more than we budgeted" is very often "here is what fits that budget" rather than a discount. Reducing what is delivered protects the rate, which protects every future negotiation with this client and everyone they refer.
- **Number of revision rounds, and what counts as one.** The single commonest source of unpriced work.
- **What happens when the client changes their mind**, which is a change order and a new number rather than a favour.
- **Payment terms.** When invoices go out, how long they take, whether there are stage payments. Long terms are a real cost and shortening them is often easier to agree than raising the fee.
- **A deposit before starting.** Also the strongest available protection against the client who does not pay.
- **A kill fee**, so a cancelled project after the schedule was held does not cost nothing to cancel.
- **Timeline.** A longer deadline is cheap for the user and sometimes free for the client, and a rush is a legitimate premium.
- **Rights and usage.** What the client may do with the work, where, for how long. Buying more should cost more, and the difference between a licence and a full transfer of ownership is frequently the largest number in the whole deal and the least discussed.
- **Credit, portfolio use, and a reference**, which cost the client nothing and have real value.
- **Expenses, who pays for what, and whose tools and licences are used.**
- **Late payment terms**, which mostly matter for being present rather than for being invoked.

Where the user's problem is that a piece of work keeps growing, the fix is nearly always in this list rather than in the rate.

## Step 4: Plan the concessions in order, and never give one away free

Decide before the conversation what will be traded, in what sequence, and what comes back for each. A concession made in the room without a plan is made at the worst possible moment.

- **Rank every lever from step 3**, and do it with a method rather than by feel. For each one ask two questions and write both answers down: what does giving this cost the user in money, time, or risk, and what does the client actually get from it. Most levers fall out immediately. The ones cheap to give and valuable to receive are the trading stock and go first. The ones expensive to give and worth little to the client should never have been on the table, and they are where freelancers quietly bleed, because they get conceded as goodwill. The ones expensive to give and genuinely valuable to the client are the real currency and are what a concession should be paid for with. And the ones cheap for both should simply be agreed early, because they build the sense that the conversation is going somewhere.
- **Attach a return to every concession**, stated in the same sentence. Something moves down, something moves up. A discount given for nothing teaches the client that the first number was not real, and every subsequent number from this user will be treated the same way.
- **Decide the smallest step you will move in.** A first concession that is large signals the number was invented and invites another push.
- **Know the last thing you will give**, and know what is not available at any price.

Then write down the three or four outcomes that are acceptable, ranked, so any of them can be recognised in the room. People fail negotiations by not noticing that they have already got a version of what they wanted.

## Step 5: Write the actual sentences

This is the part that makes the difference and the part most preparation leaves out. The user knows what they want; the hard bit is saying it out loud to someone who sounds unimpressed.

Draft the real words for:

- **Stating the number.** Flat, no apology, no justification volunteered. A rate that arrives wrapped in reasons invites the reasons to be argued with, and the price is not the beginning of the negotiation, it is a statement of what the work costs.
- **The silence afterwards.** Say the number and stop. This is genuinely hard and worth naming as the single most common unforced error: filling the pause with a discount nobody asked for.
- **The response to "that is more than we had in mind."** This is not a rejection, it is the standard next move, and it is usually the scope reply rather than a lower number.
- **The response to a request for a discount with nothing offered back**, which is a trade rather than a refusal.
- **The response to "what would it take."** A real answer, not a flinch.
- **Saying no**, in a sentence, without over-explaining. Over-explanation is where people talk themselves back into a bad deal.
- **The close**: what is agreed, in writing, before work starts.

Write these in the user's own register, plain enough to say under pressure. The properties that make them work are consistent: short enough to say in one breath, no justification attached, no apology, and where a choice is being handed back, ending on the question rather than trailing off. The ones that answer an objection should give the client a decision to make rather than a position to argue with, since a question returns the problem to their side of the table and a statement invites another push. And put in the one line that buys time — that they will look at it properly and come back — because almost nothing has to be answered in the room, and agreeing on the spot is how people end up with terms they did not intend.

## Step 6: Raising rates with an existing client

A different and harder problem than quoting a new one, and worth handling separately because the standard advice misfires.

The leverage is the switching cost. A client who has to replace someone who already knows their business faces a real expense in time and risk, which is usually much larger than the increase being asked for. That is the argument, and it does not need to be said out loud to be true.

Practical points:

- **Give notice, and attach it to a boundary**: a date, a new project, a renewal, the start of a period. An increase applied to work already agreed is a different and much worse conversation than one that starts with the next thing.
- **Say the new rate rather than asking for it.** A request invites a decision; a notification invites a negotiation about implementation, which is where the user wants to be.
- **Do not justify with personal costs.** The client is not buying the user's rent. Justify with the market, the work, or nothing at all, and nothing at all is usually strongest.
- **Decide in advance what happens if they say no**, because this is the branch people avoid thinking about and then face live. Staying at the old rate for a defined period, reducing scope to match, or leaving are all real answers, and having chosen one is what makes the conversation survivable.
- **Expect it to go better than feared.** The usual outcome is agreement, sometimes after a delay. The unusual outcome is worth planning for anyway.

## Step 6b: When the negotiation is the wrong lever

Sometimes the rate is not the problem and winning the negotiation does not fix anything. Say so, once, rather than producing an excellent prep sheet for the wrong question.

The main case is concentration. Where one client is a large share of someone's income, the weak position in the negotiation is a symptom, and no wording repairs it: the client does not have to do anything unreasonable for the arrangement to be dangerous, since a change on their side that has nothing to do with the user ends the income anyway. Two things follow. Concentration should raise the price rather than lower it, because the user is carrying business risk on the client's behalf, and that is a fair thing to say. And the highest-value action available before the conversation is not better preparation, it is landing anything else, because it changes the position rather than the presentation, and it will be audible in a way that cannot be performed.

The other cases worth naming: work that is unprofitable at any rate the client would ever pay, where the answer is to stop rather than to negotiate; and a relationship where the recurring problem is late payment or scope rather than price, where a higher number solves nothing.

Say this briefly and then give them the prep anyway. The conversation is still happening.

## Step 6c: When the deal requires spending money to fulfil it

The file above assumes someone selling their own time, where the worst outcome is being underpaid. Where a contract requires hiring, buying equipment, taking a lease, or turning existing work away, the risk changes kind and several things above stop being true.

- **Payment terms stop being a lever and become a condition.** Long terms plus staff paid weekly or monthly means financing the client's work for months before the first payment arrives. Work out the size of that gap explicitly — the recurring cost multiplied by the months before money lands — and check the money exists. If it does not, the terms are not something to trade against price, they are something the deal cannot proceed without, and it should be said that plainly.
- **Price the work being displaced, not just the work being won.** Turning down existing customers to take a larger contract can reduce total profit while tripling the workload, particularly where the displaced work carried the better margin. Do that subtraction before anything else, because it occasionally produces the real answer.
- **Displaced work does not come back.** Customers released go somewhere else and stay there, so releasing them wholesale converts a reversible decision into an irreversible one. Phasing out the worst-margin ones and keeping a core is nearly always the better shape.
- **Propose a smaller start with a scheduled ramp.** This is usually the strongest single move available: it de-risks the user's side, costs the client little, and is easy for them to agree to, because a supplier who collapses partway through is their problem too.
- **Check the exit is symmetric.** A long fixed commitment on one side against a short notice period on the other is frequently the worst term in the document and is more dangerous than the price.
- **A fixed price over a long term needs a review point**, since the costs underneath it will move.
- **Verify that any liability being accepted is actually insurable**, by asking the insurer about the specific wording rather than assuming. An uninsurable obligation is not something to negotiate down; it is a reason not to sign.

## Step 7: What to send to a lawyer

Not a legal review, but a flag on the clauses that carry real exposure, so the user knows what is worth an hour of someone's time:

- Anything transferring ownership of work outright rather than licensing it, particularly where it covers pre-existing material or tools the user reuses
- Indemnity clauses, which can create liability far larger than the fee
- Any cap on liability, or the absence of one
- Non-compete and non-solicit terms, and how long they run
- Terms about what happens on cancellation, and whether work done is paid for
- Anything about confidentiality that survives the engagement indefinitely
- Automatic renewal, and how notice is given

Say that the cost of that hour is small against the size of these, and that the ones creating unlimited exposure are the priority. Do not opine on enforceability.

## Step 8: Deliver

Give back a page they can have open during the call:

1. The walk-away number and what happens if they walk
2. The ranked outcomes, so a good one is recognised
3. The levers, marked with what to trade and what to keep
4. The sentences, in their words
5. The two or three things to find out before the conversation
6. Anything to get looked at legally
7. What to confirm in writing afterwards, and by when

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Preparing a number without a walk-away, so the number moves under the first push.
- Building the walk-away from what feels acceptable rather than from what the work costs.
- Negotiating only on price, when scope, rights, terms, and timeline are where the value is.
- Treating "that is more than we budgeted" as a rejection rather than as the standard next move.
- Discounting without taking anything out, which teaches the client that the price was never real.
- Justifying a rate with personal circumstances.
- Filling the silence after saying the number.
- Agreeing in the room when nothing required an answer that day.
- Preparing an argument when the person on the other side has no authority to agree.
- Applying a rate increase retroactively to work already agreed, instead of attaching it to a boundary.
- Supplying a market rate you do not actually have, which a nervous user will repeat and be corrected on.
- Building the whole plan on a walk-away that was never calculated, without saying that is what happened.
- Preparing an excellent negotiation for a problem that is really about one client being too large a share of the income.
- Timing an ask to the contract date when the budget was fixed months earlier.
- Ranking concessions by feel, so the expensive-to-give and worthless-to-them levers get conceded as goodwill.
- Treating a deal that requires hiring or capital as a larger version of a freelance job, when the risk is cash and commitment rather than rate.
- Trading away payment terms on a contract that cannot be funded without them.
- Releasing existing customers wholesale for a contract that has not started.
- Ruling on whether a contract clause is enforceable.
