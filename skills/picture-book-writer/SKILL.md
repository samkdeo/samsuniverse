# Picture Book Writer

Drafts picture book manuscripts with the structural conventions that make them work read aloud: short page-by-page text blocks, rhythm, repetition, and pacing suited to a specific age band. This is not a general children's-story skill. It produces manuscript text formatted the way a picture book editor expects it, with page breaks and word counts per page, not a prose short story.

## Step 1: Capture the essentials

Get these before drafting. Ask only for what's missing, and don't re-ask for anything the user already gave you.

1. Target age band. This drives everything else.
   - 0-3 (board book): 100-300 words total, one sentence or phrase per page, heavy repetition, concrete nouns only, no subplot
   - 4-6 (picture book): 500-1,000 words total, 1-3 sentences per page, simple cause-and-effect plot, can handle mild suspense
   - 7-9 (early reader or older picture book): 1,000-2,000 words total, can carry a real subplot, longer sentences, some vocabulary stretch is fine
2. Page count, which follows from the age band rather than a single default.
   - 0-3 board book: 12 to 24 pages, commonly 12, 16, 20, or 24. Board books are bound differently from picture books, so the multiples-of-8 rule below doesn't apply to them. Do not default to 32 here: at 100-300 words, 32 pages gives you 3 to 9 words a page, which is not a book, it's a caption set.
   - 4-6: 32 pages, the standard trade format. Picture books at this size print in multiples of 8.
   - 7-9: 32 pages, or 40 for a text-heavy story.
   Say which you picked and why, so the user can overrule it.
3. Core idea: the character, the want, and the obstacle. If the user only has a vague theme ("a story about sharing"), help them find a concrete character and situation before drafting. A theme on its own doesn't make scenes.
4. Tone: funny, gentle bedtime, adventurous, silly-rhyming, and so on. When it isn't stated, infer it from the request. "Bedtime story" is a tone. Don't ask.

An age sitting on a band seam, most often 3, 6, or 7, goes to the band that matches what the child can follow rather than the number. A 3-year-old takes the top of the 0-3 range rather than the middle, so aim for 150 to 200 words instead of 120, and keep the one-line-per-page shape.

Where the word target and the one-line shape fight each other, the shape wins. A 24-page board book at one line per page cannot honestly carry 250 words, and padding each page to two sentences to reach a number breaks the predictability the form runs on. Come in under the target, say you did, and offer to add a beat rather than thickening the existing ones.

### Don't interrogate anyone

Two cases, and neither of them is a question round.

A fully formed idea: confirm the age band and page count, state your other assumptions, and draft.

A vague one-liner, which is the more common case and usually arrives as a subject plus an age and nothing else: invent the character, the want, and the obstacle, draft immediately, and surface every invented choice as a sign-off item in the Step 4 summary. A parent asking at bedtime should not get six questions about age band, page count, character, want, obstacle, and tone. Inventing and flagging is faster for them and easier to correct than answering a form, because they can react to a draft.

Ask a real question only when the answer would change the whole book and you cannot infer it, which is rarer than it feels.

## Step 2: Pick a structural pattern

Match the pattern to the age band and the idea rather than defaulting to one shape every time.

- Cumulative tale (0-6): each page adds to a repeated refrain, as in "The House That Jack Built." Best for 0-3.
- Problem, escalating attempts, resolution (4-9): the standard picture-book arc. The character wants something, tries two or three things that don't work, and then the real solution lands, usually because of something established earlier. Make it a callback, not a new element out of nowhere.
- Day-in-the-life or circular (0-6): starts and ends in the same place, like getting ready for bed. Good for bedtime books, low stakes, reassuring.
- Question and answer (0-4): each spread poses a question and the next spread answers it.
- Accumulate and reverse (7-9): the character builds a case, a collection, or a conviction across a series of spreads, and the ending turns it over. Use it when the engine is a character who is confidently wrong, or when the payoff is ironic rather than triumphant. The standard attempts arc does not describe this shape, and forcing it on one flattens the joke.
- Braided (7-9): the main plot and a subplot alternate by spread and converge at the turning point.

## Step 2b: Running a subplot at 7-9

The top band allows one subplot and it needs handling, since an unmanaged one either vanishes or eats the book.

Give it roughly a tenth of the word budget, carried by a recurring character who wants something small and adjacent. It appears in perhaps a third of the spreads, never gets a spread to itself, and rides inside scenes the main plot was already having.

It has to converge. A subplot that simply stops is worse than none, so its payoff belongs in the final spread or the one before, ideally as the thing that lets the ending land. When it can be cut without changing the ending, it was decoration.

Say in the sign-off summary what the subplot costs in words and what is lost by cutting it, so the user can make that call rather than discovering it late.

## Step 3: Draft page-by-page

Format every draft as spreads, not paragraphs. A spread is what the reader sees when the book is open, and one page-turn is usually one beat of story, not one sentence per line unless the age band calls for it.

At 7-9 the word budget and the spread count fight each other: 1,000 to 2,000 words across 14 to 16 spreads is 65 to 140 words a spread, and the top of that range is a wall of text on a picture book page. What gives is the illustrated format. Above roughly 1,400 words the book is drifting toward an illustrated early reader with more and smaller pages, so either hold the total near the floor of the band or tell the user the format is changing under them.

Work out the unit and the count first. Front matter takes the opening pages in both formats. A 32-page picture book runs its story across roughly 14 to 16 spreads. A 24-page board book runs pages 3 to 24, which is 22 story pages, or about 11 spreads if you work in spreads at all.

At 0-3 the unit is usually the single page rather than the spread, because one line facing one image is the whole design. Use the single-page format there:

```
**Page 3:**
[one line as it appears on the page]

**Page 4:**
...
```

At 4-9, use the spread format below.

Output format:

```
**Page 1-2 (spread 1):**
[text as it would appear on the page]
(illustration note: one line on what's happening visually, only if it isn't obvious from the text. Two or three across a whole manuscript is right. More than that is art-directing an illustrator who hasn't been hired yet. The exception is any spread where the picture carries a beat the text deliberately withholds. An ending the words refuse to state is the clearest case, and there the note is not optional, because without it the book has no ending. The same applies wherever the joke lives in the gap between what the text claims and what the picture shows, which in an accumulate-and-reverse comedy can be several spreads. Note those and say why, rather than staying under a count.)

**Page 3-4 (spread 2):**
...
```

Rules while drafting:

- Every page has to earn a page-turn. If nothing changes, whether that's new information, new emotion, or a new visual, merge the page into the one next to it.
- Text and image split the work. Don't describe in text what the illustration will obviously show. Skip "The bear was orange and wore a hat" if that's just what the picture looks like. Text carries plot, dialogue, and interior feeling. Pictures carry setting and appearance.
- Repeat on purpose, at every band. What changes with age is the job it does, not whether you use it. Under 7 a repeated line ("But the little seed said, 'Not yet.'") gives a child something to predict and join in on. At 7-9 the same device becomes a runner: a line that recurs, accretes meaning, and pays off by flipping in the last spread. Dropping repetition at the top band because it looks like a younger-reader device throws away the strongest structural tool available for comedy and for irony.
- Read every line aloud in your head, or literally if the tool supports it, and cut any sentence that trips on itself. Prefer short words and concrete nouns over abstract ones, especially under age 6.
- When the tone is funny, the humour needs a mechanism, not just a light touch. The reliable ones at this age: a repeated line that flips its meaning at the end, an escalating series of increasingly absurd claims, a character who is wrong with total confidence, an adult who does not notice what the child notices, and a gross-but-safe detail. Pick one and build it in, rather than hoping the prose comes out funny. One mechanism carries a short book; a longer one at 7-9 wants three working together, typically a runner that recurs, an escalation that raises the absurdity each time, and a reversal that turns the whole thing over at the end.
- When the joke is that a character is wrong, protect them at the reversal. A book can prove a child mistaken without making them the butt of it, and the difference is where the last beat puts them. Do not end on adults being amused at them, and do not have an adult explain that being wrong is fine, which implies someone thought they were stupid. Instead let the correction come with credit for something the character genuinely did well, ideally the thing that made them wrong in the first place: the thoroughness, the noticing, the willingness to write down the answer they didn't like. The reader should finish liking them more than at the start, not less.
- Watch for the preachy middle, not just the preachy last line. A user asking for "funny, not preachy" is usually protecting against a moral in the final spread, but the more common leak is a middle spread where an adult character explains the theme kindly to the child. Cut that scene.
- End on the emotional beat, not the explanation. Picture books trust the picture and the white space, so don't over-explain the moral in text, especially for 4-9. If there's a lesson, let the character's actions carry it.

## Step 4: Deliver with a page and word count summary

Give the manuscript a title, and say it's a placeholder unless the user supplied one.

Every draft ends with:

- Total word count against the target range for the age band, flagged if you're over or under
- Page count used against the count requested
- One line naming any creative liberty the user should sign off on, such as a character trait or an ending choice

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Writing adult-register vocabulary or sentence structure and calling it "simple." Read it back as though a 4-year-old has to sit through it at a normal reading pace.
- Padding to hit a word count instead of cutting a weak page.
- Solving the problem in the last spread with a character or object that was never set up. The solution should feel inevitable in hindsight, not random.
- Padding a board book to hit a word target, at the cost of the one-line-per-page rhythm the form depends on.

## If the user wants to iterate

Ask which spreads to revise instead of regenerating the whole manuscript. Picture book text is short enough that surgical edits are usually faster, and they preserve what's already working.
