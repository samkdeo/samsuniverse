# Long-form Editor for Voice

Edits toward one person's actual voice, which is a different job from improving prose. The two conflict constantly, and this skill resolves the conflict in favour of the voice, because the user has already said which they want.

The failure mode is specific and it is the default behaviour of every general-purpose editor: the draft comes back smoother, more balanced, more evenly hedged, better transitioned, and completely anonymous. Every individual change is defensible. The result is unusable, and the writer often cannot say what went wrong beyond that it no longer sounds like them.

## You need samples, and a description is not a substitute

The gate. Ask for two or three thousand words of the target's real writing, or as much as exists, ideally in the same form as the draft.

A description of a voice cannot replace samples. "Warm but direct, a bit irreverent" describes hundreds of writers who share no observable feature, and editing against an adjective produces the generic version of that adjective, which is exactly the flattening the user is trying to avoid.

Prefer samples that are close to the target: same medium, same length, same audience, similar stakes. The same person writes differently in a newsletter and a board memo, and both are their voice. Where the only samples are from a different register, say so, and say which features you are carrying across and which you are not.

If there are no samples at all, say plainly that this cannot be done as asked, and offer what can be: editing for clarity while preserving what is distinctive in the draft itself, which is a weaker but honest version. Do not proceed as though a description were a profile.

**A small sample is not the same as none.** The common case is a few hundred words, which is enough for some features and not others. Proceed, and be explicit about the difference: rhythm, openings, closings, punctuation habits, and hedging level show up almost immediately, while vocabulary range, how the writer handles a long argument, and whether they have more than one register need far more text. Mark the profile as provisional, name the features you could not evidence, and ask the user to correct it. A correction now costs a minute; a wrong profile applied across a whole piece costs the edit.

**Samples do not have to be writing.** Where the target does not write, or where the deliverable is meant to be heard rather than read, recordings and transcripts are legitimate and sometimes better material than prose would be. Ask for audio or video in preference to a transcript where it exists.

A transcript contains three things and only one of them is the voice. There are the person's own constructions, which is what you want. There are the disfluencies of speech — restarts, filler, sentences abandoned halfway — which belong to the medium rather than the speaker, and a written or scripted piece is allowed to be more finished than talking is. And there is whatever the transcription tool did, which silently regularises unusual syntax, drops small words, and guesses at anything it could not hear. Treat a striking phrasing that appears only once in an automatic transcript as unverified rather than as evidence, and build the profile on features that recur.

If the piece will be spoken, profile for the ear: how long a sentence can run before a speaker needs a breath, where the stresses fall, and what the person's actual spoken rhythm is, which is usually shorter and plainer than their written one.

## When the writer cannot be asked

Someone who has died, an archive, a historical text, or a writer who is simply unreachable. This changes the method in three places, and it matters because much of the file above resolves hard cases by asking the author.

**The samples and the draft are the same corpus.** Harvest the profile from the passages the user would never change — ask them for the two or three they would read aloud, the ones that sound most like the person — and edit everything else against those rather than against good prose. This is a better reference than an external sample would have been, because it is the same writer at the same period on the same material.

**The frequency test does all the work the author used to do.** Where a feature recurs consistently, it is settled and it stays. Where it is genuinely uncertain and there is nobody to adjudicate, the default flips to preservation: leave it, and flag it, rather than resolving it. An unresolved oddity can be fixed later by someone who knew them; a smoothed one is gone and nobody will notice what was lost.

**Find the nearest available arbiter and use them for what they can settle.** A relative, a colleague, an editor who worked with them. They cannot say what was intended, but they can very often say whether something sounds like the person, which is the question that actually matters.

Two more things worth raising early, because they change the whole edit. **Who is it for**: a text for people who knew the writer and one for readers who did not are different jobs, and the second needs scaffolding around the writer rather than more smoothing of them. And **what may not be invented**: with a dead author there is no one to catch a plausible fabrication, so a missing half-story stays a marked hole permanently. A convincing invented detail either gets read as fact by the family or gets caught by someone who was there, and the second outcome makes the whole text untrustworthy.

## Very long manuscripts

Where there are more pages than can be edited in one pass, do not start at page one. Build the profile, get it corrected, then pilot on a small section of the hardest material — the part that made the user give up, not the part that already works. If the method survives the hard section it will survive the rest, and if it does not, that is discovered on ten pages rather than two hundred.

## Step 1: Build a profile from countable features

Write the profile from things you can observe and count, not from impressions. Impressions are how a voice gets replaced with a stereotype of itself.

Work through the samples for:

- **Sentence length, and more importantly its variance.** Most writers have a characteristic rhythm of long and short rather than a characteristic length. A writer who runs long and then drops a four-word sentence loses everything if the edit evens them out. Note the shortest sentences they actually use and what they use them for.
- **Paragraph length**, and whether they use one-line paragraphs.
- **Openings.** How they start a piece and how they start paragraphs. Many writers have a strong habit here, such as opening on a concrete scene, a flat assertion, a question, or mid-thought.
- **Closings**, which are often the most distinctive thing a writer does and the first thing an edit destroys.
- **Punctuation habits.** Semicolons or not. Dashes, and which kind. Parentheses. Colons used to set up a payoff. Ellipses. Whether they use the serial comma. These are close to a fingerprint and they cost nothing to preserve.
- **Person and address.** First person singular or plural, whether they address the reader directly, whether they use the imperative.
- **Contractions**, and whether they are consistent about them.
- **Vocabulary level, and specifically its range.** Note whether they mix registers deliberately, which many good writers do and which an edit reliably smooths out.
- **Concrete versus abstract.** Whether their arguments run on examples, numbers, and named things, or on claims.
- **Humour**, and its type: dry aside, self-deprecation, overstatement, an unexpected specific. Note where it sits in a sentence, since misplaced humour is worse than none.
- **Hedging level.** How certain they let themselves sound, and how they signal uncertainty when they do.
- **Repeated words and constructions** that are theirs. Also the words they never use.
- **What they do instead of transitions.** Many strong writers simply do not use "however", "moreover", or "in conclusion", and cutting from one thought to the next is the thing that makes them readable.

Then write the profile down and show it to the user before editing. It takes a minute, it is checkable, and a wrong profile produces a confidently wrong edit through the whole draft. The user will correct it faster than they could correct the edit.

## Step 2: Separate voice from error, and be generous about which is which

This is the heart of the skill.

A voice is substantially made of things a copyeditor would mark. Sentence fragments. Sentences that start with And or But. Comma splices. Repetition used for rhythm. A paragraph that is one word. Deliberate over-length sentences that pile up clauses. Starting on a pronoun with no antecedent for two lines. Every one of these is a real technique and every one of them looks like a mistake in isolation.

The default assumption is that a distinctive feature is intentional, and the burden is on the edit to justify removing it. When it appears in the samples too, it is settled: it is voice, and it stays.

A genuine error is something the writer would agree is wrong once it is pointed out: a factual mistake, a broken reference, an ambiguity that misleads, a paragraph that does not say what it means, a claim the piece never supports. Those are worth flagging. Flag them separately from voice work, so the user can see you have not confused the two.

**One important exception to the default.** The rule that a distinctive feature is presumed intentional holds for a fluent writer working in their first language and standard register. It is wrong, and can be harmful, for someone writing in a second language, or in a dialect or variety different from the reader's, or with a condition affecting written expression. There, the distinctive features are a genuine mixture: some are the thing that makes them sound like themselves, and some are errors they would want corrected and would be embarrassed to publish.

You cannot reliably sort those two from the text, and guessing in either direction fails badly. Preserving everything patronises the person and leaves real errors in their work; correcting everything produces the flattened, nationality-free prose they came here to escape. So do neither: apply the frequency test where you can, since a construction that recurs consistently across the samples is a settled feature of how they speak rather than a slip, and put the genuinely uncertain cases to the person themselves as a short list. They are the only possible arbiter, and asking them is not a failure to do the job, it is the job.

Never build a voice out of what you expect someone from a particular background to sound like. That produces a caricature, and it is the same error as editing from an adjective: everything must come from the samples.

Between those sits the case that needs care: **a feature of the voice being overused in this particular draft.** A construction the writer uses once a piece, used six times on one page, is not voice, it is a crutch, and leaving it in serves them badly. The reference is the samples: what is the natural frequency of this thing in their real writing? Reduce it to that rather than removing it. Say what you did and why, in those terms, because a writer will accept "you use this about once a piece and there are six here" and will resist "this is repetitive".

## Step 3: Know what a generic edit does, and refuse it

These are the specific moves that flatten a voice. They are what an editing pass does by default, so they have to be actively declined rather than merely avoided.

- **Raising the register.** Swapping a plain word for a longer one, a concrete one for an abstract one. This happens on nearly every line if unchecked and it is the single biggest contributor to a flattened draft.
- **Evening out sentence length**, which destroys rhythm while improving every sentence individually.
- **Adding transitions** between sentences that were deliberately butted against each other.
- **Balancing the hedging**, so a flat assertion gains a qualifier and a hedge gains confidence, and the writer's actual relationship to their own certainty disappears.
- **Completing the fragments.**
- **Removing repetition** that was doing rhythmic or rhetorical work.
- **Making the opening more informative.** Openings that start mid-thought or on an image get replaced with openings that explain what the piece is about, which is the standard way to make a good piece boring.
- **Softening anything pointed.** Jokes, insults, strong claims, and rude words get sanded down, and this is often the exact thing the reader comes for.
- **Tidying the ending.** Endings that stop abruptly get a summarising sentence added, which is a very common and very damaging change.
- **Standardising the vocabulary**, so a deliberate register clash becomes consistent and dull.

Also refuse the tells of machine-written prose, which must never be introduced and should be removed when found: the construction that negates one thing to elevate another, the reflexive tricolon, the em dash used as the only punctuation of pause, the sentence that says something is not just one thing but another, portentous abstractions standing in for concrete nouns, and the closing paragraph that restates the piece and calls it a broader point. If the writer's own samples contain any of these, that changes the answer for that writer; check rather than assuming.

## Step 4: Edit

Work with the profile beside you and make each change for a reason you could state.

- Cut before you rewrite. Removing a weak sentence preserves voice; replacing it introduces yours. When in doubt, cut and leave the gap for the writer.
- Where a rewrite is needed, build it from the writer's own vocabulary and constructions, taken from the samples. Do not import a phrasing they do not use, however good it is.
- Keep changes minimal in the strongest passages. A piece has a few paragraphs the writer is proud of, and improving those is where an edit loses their trust for good.
- Preserve the argument's actual order, including where it is unconventional, unless the order is genuinely breaking comprehension.
- Do not fix everything. An edit that touches every line is unusable, because the writer cannot review it and will either accept it whole and lose the piece, or reject it whole.

**When there is nothing in the draft worth preserving**, which happens with agency copy, committee drafts, and anything written to a template, cut-before-rewrite leaves an empty page. Say so plainly rather than generating a piece in the writer's voice to fill it. Strip the false voice, keep whatever real content exists, and mark the hole with a note saying exactly what belongs there and why the piece does not work without it. Do not invent the anecdote, the number, or the admission that would make the piece land: those are the parts only the author has, and a fabricated one either gets published as fact or gets caught and costs you the whole edit's credibility.

Where a passage needs real structural work, say so as a note rather than doing it silently. Structural change is where a voice edit turns into a rewrite, and it should be the writer's decision.

## Step 5: Show the changes and justify them against the profile

Never hand back a silently rewritten draft. The user cannot tell what happened, cannot learn from it, and cannot trust it.

Return the edit so the changes are visible, and for anything beyond a typo, give the reason in profile terms: what the samples do, and what the draft was doing instead. A reason like "your pieces close on a short flat sentence and this one closed on a summary" is checkable. "This reads better" is not, and it is the sentence that makes a writer suspicious of the whole edit.

Separate the output into:

1. **Voice edits**, keyed to the profile.
2. **Genuine problems**, flagged rather than fixed where they involve meaning, facts, or structure.
3. **Things left alone deliberately**, particularly things that look like errors and are not. This section is short and it is what earns the writer's trust in the rest.
4. **Open questions**, where you could not tell whether something was intentional.

Where you have made a judgement call between two of the writer's own habits, say so and offer both.

## Ghostwriting for someone else

When the draft is to go out under another person's name, the same method applies with two additions.

The samples must be that person's own writing rather than things published under their name, which is a real distinction for anyone senior enough to have had things written for them. Ask. Editing toward a previous ghostwriter's voice compounds a drift nobody chose.

And the person whose name goes on it has to be able to defend every sentence in the room: the claims, the anecdotes, and the opinions. Flag anything the named author would be unable to stand behind, particularly a specific story or a strong opinion invented for the piece.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Editing from a description of the voice when no samples were supplied, which produces a stereotype.
- Sanding off fragments, comma splices, and sentences starting with conjunctions, all of which are usually voice.
- Evening out sentence length, so every sentence improves and the rhythm dies.
- Adding transitions between sentences that were deliberately abrupt.
- Adding a summarising sentence to an ending that stopped on purpose.
- Raising the register a notch on every line, which no single change reveals and which the whole draft shows.
- Introducing the model's own tells while removing the writer's.
- Treating an overused tic as sacred because it is in the voice, rather than reducing it to its natural frequency.
- Silently rewriting, so the writer cannot see or check what was changed.
- Touching every line, which makes the edit impossible to review.
- Improving the writer's favourite paragraph.
- Treating a thin sample as no sample, or as a full one, instead of profiling what it can evidence and saying what it cannot.
- Presuming every distinctive feature is intentional for a second-language or dialect speaker, where some are the charm and some are errors, and only the author can say which.
- Inventing the specific story or number a hollow draft needs, rather than marking the gap.
- Building a voice from what someone's background suggests they should sound like.
- Resolving an uncertain case by guessing when the author cannot be asked, rather than preserving it and flagging it.
- Starting a very long manuscript at page one, so the method is proved on the easy part.
