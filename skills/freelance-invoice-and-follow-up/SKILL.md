# Freelance Invoice and Follow-up

Covers two things that are really one thing: an invoice built so that it cannot easily be delayed, and a chase sequence that escalates on a schedule rather than on how brave the sender feels that morning.

The premise worth stating first, because it changes the tone of everything that follows: chasing an overdue invoice is not an imposition, an awkward favour, or a relationship risk. It is a routine part of a commercial relationship, the client's own finance department does it, and the sender is currently providing an unsecured, interest-free loan they never agreed to. Most of the difficulty here is not knowing the words; it is believing that asking is legitimate. Say so once, and then be practical.

It is not legal advice and not debt collection, but it says clearly where each begins, because knowing the next step exists is what makes the earlier steps calm.

## The first invoice

Someone sending their first one needs the artefact, not a checklist, and their real question is usually whether it will look amateurish. Answer that first: nothing about a plain, correct, unapologetic invoice reads as unprofessional, and the things that do are hedging, apologising, and vagueness about money.

Give them the document. The structure that works:

```
INVOICE
[Their name or trading name, address, email, phone]

Invoice number       [a number, and start a sequence]
Invoice date         [date]
Payment due          [an actual date, not a period]

Bill to              [the client's full legal entity, not the trading name]
                     [their address]
                     [contact name, and accounts payable]
PO number            [if they use them]

Description                                          Amount
[the work, in the words the client used for it]      [ ]
                                          Total due  [ ]

[tax line, or a statement of tax status, as applicable]

Payment by bank transfer: [account details]
Payment reference: [the invoice number]
Payment terms: [as agreed]
Questions about this invoice: [phone number]
```

Then the covering email, which is three lines: what is attached, the amount and the due date, and one question asking whether it needs to go to anyone else in accounts as well. That question is the highest-value sentence in the whole message, because it catches the wrong-recipient problem while there is still time to fix it. No apology, no "no rush", and no explanation of how much they enjoyed the work used as a cushion for the money — warmth is fine, but as its own sentence rather than as padding around the number.

**Where no terms were agreed**, which is the normal state for a first invoice, the user has to choose them now rather than leave the invoice open-ended. Pick a period, state it as a date, and keep it short: a first-time freelancer with no leverage and no contract is better served by a near date they can chase against than by a generous one that leaves them waiting without recourse. Then say that terms get agreed in writing before the work next time, which is the actual fix.

**On tax, do not assert the rules.** Whether tax must be charged depends on registration status, on the thresholds and rules of a particular jurisdiction, and sometimes on where the client is, and all of those change. Say what governs rather than what the answer is: that tax is charged only if the user is registered to charge it, that registration is usually mandatory above a threshold they should check with their tax authority, and that displaying a registration number they do not hold is a real offence rather than a formatting choice. Never state a threshold, a rate, or a registration figure from memory — send them to the official source, which takes them two minutes and cannot be wrong. Where they are not registered, a single line stating that on the invoice pre-empts a query from the client's finance team.

## Step 1: Make the invoice hard to delay

Most late payment is not refusal. It is friction, and the friction is nearly always at the client's end and invisible to the sender: the invoice went to the wrong person, it lacked a reference their system requires, it arrived after the cut-off for the payment run, or it is sitting waiting for an approval nobody has been asked for.

So before anything about chasing, check the invoice removes every excuse:

- **The right recipient.** Accounts payable, not just the day-to-day contact, and both where possible. An invoice sitting in a project manager's inbox is not in the system at all.
- **Whatever reference their process requires.** A purchase order number is the big one: in many organisations an invoice without a valid PO cannot be paid at all, no matter who wants to pay it, and this single item causes an enormous share of very late invoices. Get the PO before starting work, not after.
- **A clear due date as a date**, not a number of days. "Payment due 14 October" removes an entire class of dispute about when the clock started.
- **Line items that match what was agreed**, in the client's own language for it. An invoice whose description does not match the purchase order or the brief gets queried, and a query resets the clock.
- **Bank details, and everything their finance system needs**: a reference, a tax or company registration number, the correct legal entity being billed, which is frequently not the trading name on the emails.
- **How to raise a problem**, so that a small query becomes a phone call rather than a silence.

Then find out their actual process. Ask directly and early: who approves it, who pays it, when the payment runs happen, and what has to be true for an invoice to be included. Someone who knows the payment run is on the 25th and the cut-off is the 15th can time their invoicing and remove weeks of waiting, permanently, with no confrontation involved.

Invoice promptly, because an invoice sent late tells the client that the sender is relaxed about money, and that belief is expensive to reverse.

## Step 2: Set the terms so there is something to enforce

Agreed in advance and written down, or the chasing later has nothing to stand on:

- **Payment terms**, as a specific period, agreed before work starts. Not assumed.
- **A deposit for new clients and for anything substantial.** This is the single most effective protection available, because it filters the clients who were never going to pay and it means an unpaid final invoice is not the whole fee.
- **Stage payments on anything long**, so exposure never exceeds one stage.
- **Late payment terms**, including interest, present in the contract. Their real value is being cited rather than charged.
- **What happens when payment is late**: work pauses, access is withdrawn, files are not released. Deciding this in advance, and having it in writing, is what makes it possible to do without it seeming personal.

For anything discussed rather than contracted, a short written confirmation of scope, fee, and terms is enough and is much better than nothing.

## Step 3: The sequence

The point of a sequence is that it runs on dates rather than on nerve. Decide it once, put the dates in a calendar, and follow them. Sending a chaser because you are annoyed produces the wrong tone; sending one because it is Thursday produces the right one.

The shape that works, with the timing set relative to the due date rather than to how long it feels:

1. **Before it is due.** A short, friendly note a few days ahead confirming it is scheduled and asking whether anything is needed. Not chasing — it catches the missing PO or wrong recipient while there is still time, and it is the highest-value message in the whole sequence.
2. **The day after it is due.** Immediately, and neutrally. Waiting a polite week teaches the client that the terms are decorative, and this message is the one that establishes they are not.
3. **A week later.** Slightly firmer, asking for a specific payment date rather than a status. This is the shift that matters: a request for information gets a vague answer, a request for a date gets a date or an admission that there is not one.
4. **Two weeks later.** Firmer. State the amount, the days overdue, and what happens next, and copy in accounts payable if they have not been.
5. **Around a month overdue.** Formal in tone. Reference the contract terms, state the interest position, give a deadline, and say what follows it. This is the last one that is a chaser rather than a warning.
6. **After that**, it stops being a sequence and becomes a decision. See step 6.

Escalate the recipient as well as the tone. Moving from the day-to-day contact, to accounts payable, to the contact's manager, to a director is often what actually causes payment, because it converts a thing one person is not getting round to into a thing several people can see.

Change the channel too. Email is easy to leave unanswered and a phone call is not. A single call to accounts payable asking which payment run the invoice is in resolves a great many of these, and it also identifies which stage the invoice is stuck at, which no amount of emailing will.

## Entering the sequence in the middle

The commonest real request. The invoice is long overdue, several chasers have already gone, and the user is not at step one of anything. Do not restart the sequence, and do not deliver the prevention advice as though it were the answer.

Locate them by what has already been tried rather than by elapsed days, since a message that has been sent does not count twice. Several emails to the same person is one step repeated, not four steps completed, and the sequence has effectively stalled at whatever stage it reached. Then resume at the next unused step, which is nearly always an escalation of recipient or channel rather than another message to the same inbox.

Two diagnostic points matter more here than the wording. **Repeated non-answers usually mean the contact has nothing to report** rather than that they are avoiding the sender — they have asked internally, heard nothing, and have no update they want to send. And **an invoice may not be in the client's system at all**, in which case it is not late, it is invisible, and no amount of chasing the contact will surface it. Neither can be determined from the sender's desk, which is why the first action at this stage is a phone call to accounts payable rather than a sixth email.

Rebuild the calendar from today with real dates, including the date the sequence ends and what happens then, and say plainly that the final step should not be named unless the user will actually take it.

**Where the relationship matters**, and it often does, a short private note to the contact before escalating protects it: that the invoice is going to accounts and to their manager, that it is not personal, and that it needs to reach someone who can pay it. Send it as well as the escalation, never instead of it. A contact who has been unable to help is frequently relieved rather than offended, because the escalation gives them cover too.

## When the client is a person rather than an organisation

Most of the machinery above assumes a business: accounts payable, purchase orders, payment runs, approvers, a manager to escalate to. A private individual has none of that, and applying the business playbook to them reads as bizarre and achieves nothing. What changes:

- **The diagnosis is different.** There is no invisible process to be stuck in. Silence from a private client after delivery is usually mild avoidance, money that is genuinely not there, a life event, or two people in a household each assuming the other paid it. None of that is visible from the sender's desk and none of it is fixed by another email.
- **Escalating the recipient means including everyone who owes it**, rather than going up a hierarchy. Where two people are jointly liable, address everything to both from the first message. Two people who can each see the other has been asked behave differently from one person quietly hoping it resolves itself.
- **Changing channel means the telephone**, and it works better here than in any business context, because there is no gatekeeper and the call is short.
- **The commercial late-payment statutes generally do not apply.** Interest and recovery rules written for business-to-business debts usually cannot be claimed against a consumer, so the thing to cite is whatever the user's own contract says. And consumer debt claims often have their own pre-action requirements with prescribed content and response times that must be followed before anything is issued. Say that these exist and send the user to the official source; do not attempt the detail.
- **Warmth in the call, facts in the writing.** The written record has to be re-sendable and escalatable without the tone having to change, and it cannot be if the first message was chatty.
- **Do not weaponise evidence of enjoyment.** Where the client is visibly using or displaying the work while not paying for it, that is useful evidence of delivery and acceptance and should be quietly recorded. Leading with it reads as bitter, and in a small local market it converts a debt into a reputational fight the user will lose.

## When the work has already been handed over

The strongest lever in this file — pausing work and withholding what has not been delivered — is spent the moment everything has been sent, and that is the commonest way a freelancer ends up stuck. Say so plainly rather than recommending a lever that no longer exists.

What is often still unspent: later stages, additional deliverables, source or original files, ongoing hosting or access, credits, and anything with a renewal or expiry attached. Withhold what genuinely has not been handed over. Do not retroactively remove something the client already has, which converts a clean debt into a dispute and puts the user in the wrong.

Where the contract grants rights or a licence conditional on full payment, that is a legitimate thing to state plainly at the formal stage, as what the contract says rather than as a threat.

## Step 4: How to write them

The tone rules matter more than the words, because the words follow from them.

- **No apology, ever.** "Sorry to chase" concedes that asking is an imposition, and it is the single most common flaw in these messages.
- **Short.** A long chaser reads as anxious. Three sentences is usually plenty.
- **Facts, not feelings.** The invoice number, the amount, the date it was due, the days elapsed. Facts are hard to argue with and they are not personal, which is what makes them usable in a relationship the sender wants to keep.
- **Ask for a date, not a status.** "When will this be paid?" beats "just checking in on this."
- **One clear action**, with the invoice attached again so nobody has to look for it.
- **Neutral, not passive-aggressive.** Pointed pleasantries and heavy emphasis are read exactly as intended and cost the sender the moral high ground they currently hold entirely.
- **Never threaten what you will not do.** A deadline that passes without consequence teaches the client to ignore the next one, and it costs more than it gained.

Firmer means plainer and more specific, not angrier. Later messages get shorter, not longer.

## Step 5: The standard evasions, and the answers

Each of these has a reply that moves things forward rather than accepting the delay.

- **"It's in the next payment run."** Ask which date, and confirm it in writing. Then diarise the day after. If it misses that run, the answer has been tested and the sequence moves up a step.
- **"We never received it."** Resend immediately, to accounts payable and the contact, and ask them to confirm receipt. Then ask which address it should have gone to, so it cannot happen twice.
- **"There's a query."** Deal with it the same day, because an unanswered query is the client's best reason to hold the whole amount. If the query concerns part of the invoice, ask for the undisputed part to be paid now and the rest resolved separately, which is reasonable and frequently agreed.
- **"The person who approves it is away."** Ask who covers them. There is always a delegate, and asking for them by role is what surfaces one.
- **"We're waiting on our own client to pay us."** Their cash flow is not the sender's problem and their contract is not with their client. Say it politely and hold the date.
- **Silence.** The most common of all. Change channel and change recipient rather than sending a fourth email into the same void.

Where the client is genuinely struggling, a payment plan with dates in writing is a legitimate outcome and often better than the alternative. Get it in writing, and treat a missed instalment as the end of the plan rather than the start of a new negotiation.

## Step 6: When chasing stops

There is a point where more chasers cost more than they recover, and knowing where it is makes the earlier steps calmer.

The options, roughly in order of cost:

- **Stop work and withhold anything not yet handed over**, where the contract allows it. Immediate, free, and by far the most effective lever available, which is why it belongs earlier in a freelancer's thinking than it usually does. Say what is being paused, without drama.
- **A formal letter before action.** In many jurisdictions this is a recognised step with its own conventions, and it is often the message that gets paid, precisely because it is visibly the last one before something else.
- **Statutory interest and recovery costs**, which in some places a business can claim on late commercial payments as of right. Worth the user checking what applies where they are, since it is frequently more than they assume and it is a legitimate thing to cite.
- **Small claims or the local equivalent**, which for modest sums is often designed to be used without a lawyer.
- **A collections agency**, which costs a share of the debt and generally ends the relationship.
- **Writing it off**, which is sometimes the right commercial answer and should be a decision rather than a slow fade. Where the sum is small and the client is otherwise good, this can be correct; where the client is not otherwise good, it should be paired with never working for them again.

Say that these exist and what they are for, and do not state the numbers. Interest rates, fixed recovery sums, court fees, and claim limits are all jurisdiction-specific, all change, and are exactly the kind of figure a model recalls confidently and wrongly. A user who cites a wrong rate in a formal letter damages a position that was strong, so the instruction is to name the mechanism, say where the current figure is published, and let them fetch it. Professional bodies and small business organisations usually publish the local version, and the relevant government source is authoritative.

One thing worth saying that is not jurisdiction-specific: a written record of an agreed scope and price, followed by work delivered and accepted, is generally a contract, and the absence of a signed document is a much weaker position than users fear. What a missing contract usually costs is the agreed late-payment terms and the agreed consequences, not the debt itself.

## Step 7: Prevent the repeat

After it resolves, one short review, because the same client will do the same thing again.

Ask what actually caused the delay, since the answer is usually structural and fixable: no PO, wrong recipient, an approver nobody knew about, an invoice arriving after the cut-off. Then fix that thing for next time.

Then decide the client's status honestly. A client who pays late every time is charging the sender for the privilege of working for them, and the answers are a deposit, stage payments, shorter terms, prepayment, a price that reflects it, or not working for them. Deciding on the evidence, once, is better than being surprised each time.

## Step 8: Deliver

Give back:

1. The invoice fixes, if the invoice is the problem, since this is often the whole answer.
2. The sequence with actual dates in it, calculated from the due date.
3. The next message, written and ready to send.
4. The specific thing to find out, which is usually where in their process the invoice is stuck.
5. What happens at the end of the sequence, decided now.
6. The one change that prevents it next time.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Apologising for asking to be paid.
- Waiting a polite week after the due date, which teaches the client the terms are decorative.
- Chasing when annoyed rather than on a date, which produces the wrong tone at the wrong moment.
- Emailing the same person four times instead of changing recipient and channel.
- Asking for a status update instead of a payment date.
- Accepting "it's in the next payment run" without a date and a diary note.
- Letting an unanswered query hold up the undisputed part of an invoice.
- Threatening a consequence that then does not happen.
- Passive-aggressive politeness, which forfeits the only advantage the sender has.
- Continuing to work while unpaid, when pausing is available and is the strongest lever there is.
- Chasing forever because the next step feels like a bigger deal than it is.
- Restarting the sequence for someone who is already six messages and two months in.
- Counting several emails to the same person as several steps completed.
- Stating a tax threshold, an interest rate, a fixed recovery sum, or a court fee from memory, in a document the user will send to a client.
- Handing a first-time invoicer a list of criteria when what they needed was the document.
- Sending a private individual a chaser about purchase orders, payment runs, and accounts payable.
- Recommending that work be paused when everything has already been delivered.
- Retroactively withdrawing something the client already has, which turns a clean debt into a dispute.
- Treating a repeat late payer as bad luck rather than as a client whose terms should change.
