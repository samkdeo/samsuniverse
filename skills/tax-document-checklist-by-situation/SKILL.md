# Tax Document Checklist by Situation

Produces one list: what this person needs to find, who issues each item, roughly when it becomes available, and what to do about the ones that get harder to obtain with time. The value is in being specific to their situation, because a generic checklist is simultaneously too long to be usable and missing the two items that actually apply to them.

## The boundary, and it is firm

This skill organises. It does not advise, and the line is not a formality: tax rules are jurisdiction-specific, change every year, and carry penalties, and a confident wrong number here is worse than no answer because it will be acted on.

So, never:

- State a rate, a threshold, an allowance, a deadline, or a penalty. These change annually, they are exactly what a model recalls fluently and wrongly, and they are trivially available from the tax authority.
- Say whether something is taxable, deductible, claimable, or exempt.
- Say whether someone needs to file at all.
- Calculate anything, or estimate what is owed.
- Advise on structure, timing, or anything that would change the tax rather than describe the paperwork.

**Two refinements, because the ban as stated is both too wide and too narrow.**

Too wide: a structural fact about how a system is shaped is not a changing number, and hedging around one makes the answer vague where it should be plain. That a jurisdiction's tax year does not run with the calendar, that a return covers a defined period, that separate periods exist either side of an event — these are stable, they are what makes the checklist comprehensible, and refusing to say them produces evasiveness rather than safety. Say the shape; do not say the date, the rate, or the threshold.

Do this without becoming evasive, which is the failure on this side. Naming a boundary exists and pointing at where its dates are published, in one line, is plain and useful. Circling the same fact in four hedged phrases because you are avoiding a number is worse than either saying it or looking it up, and it makes the user distrust the parts of the list that are solid. The formulation that works: state that the period is defined, that it may not be the one they assume, that confirming it takes a moment at the authority's own site, and that every document below is stamped with it.

Too narrow: the ban lists rates and deductibility, which is not actually how a model goes wrong here. The likelier failure is a confidently asserted piece of **procedural mechanics** — who receives which document and when, what an institution does or does not issue, what a form is for, which body handles what. These are fluent, plausible, specific, and frequently wrong or out of date, and they read as more authoritative than a number because they sound like administration rather than opinion. Treat them with the same discipline: say what to ask for and who to ask, rather than asserting what will be issued. "Ask their payroll what they produced for you" is right; a confident statement about what payroll must have produced is not, however reasonable it sounds.

And always:

- Frame every item as a document to obtain, not as a treatment to apply. "The statement showing interest paid" rather than "the interest you can deduct."
- Say where the authoritative answer lives, which is the tax authority's own site for the specific year, and a qualified professional for anything about treatment.
- Note when something is a question for a preparer rather than a gap in the paperwork, and mark it on the list so it gets asked rather than guessed.

Where a user asks a treatment question directly — is this deductible, do I have to declare this — answer the shape of it rather than the substance: say what document would evidence it either way, so they can arrive at the answer with the paperwork in hand, and say who can settle it. That is genuinely more useful than a guess and it is honest.

## When there is only one message

Most of the steps below are questions, and this request usually arrives as a paragraph with a deadline attached. Do not stall. Build the list from what is there, and because the stakes here are higher than in most skills, be unusually explicit about the assumptions: put them at the top rather than the bottom, state each as a thing to confirm rather than as background, and say specifically what changes if one is wrong. An assumption that would alter the whole list — the jurisdiction, the year, whether a second jurisdiction is involved — is a heading, not a footnote.

Then build the list anyway. The documents have to be gathered whatever the answers turn out to be, and gathering them is the part the user can start today.

## Step 1: Get the jurisdiction and the year first

Both, before anything else, because a checklist without them is wrong rather than approximate.

**Jurisdiction**, meaning the country and, where it matters, the state, province, or region, because sub-national filing is a separate list. Where someone has moved during the year, has income arising in one place while living in another, holds citizenship of a country that taxes on that basis, or works remotely across a border, say plainly that this is the situation where a generic checklist fails hardest and where a professional is worth the fee. Then still build the list, because the documents have to be gathered either way and gathering them is what makes the professional's hour cheap.

**The tax year in question**, which does not align with the calendar year everywhere and does not align between jurisdictions. Get the specific year, since documents are year-stamped and gathering the wrong year's paperwork is a common and infuriating waste.

If the user has not said, ask, or state the assumption prominently rather than burying it. Everything below is conditional on both.

## Step 2: Enumerate income by source, not by document

The organising move, and the reason this beats a template. Ask what money came in, from every source, and derive the paperwork from the answer rather than reading out a list of forms and hoping.

Work through the categories out loud, because prompting is what surfaces the forgotten ones:

- Employment, including any job held for part of the year and any job left
- Self-employment, freelance, contract, and consulting work, including one-off pieces
- Platform and marketplace income: selling, driving, delivering, letting, creating, streaming
- Rental income from property or from anything else let out
- Interest from savings, including accounts opened for a bonus rate and forgotten
- Dividends and distributions
- Sales of assets: shares, funds, property, crypto, anything of significant value
- Pensions, annuities, and retirement account withdrawals
- Benefits, grants, support payments, and anything received from a government body
- Trusts, estates, inheritances, and anything received as a beneficiary
- Foreign income of any kind, and any foreign account
- Royalties, licensing, and residual payments
- Anything paid in cash, in goods, in equity, or in cryptocurrency
- Anything received that the user is unsure counts, which is the most productive question on the list

For each source that exists, note **the document that evidences it, who issues it, and whether it arrives automatically or has to be requested.** That last distinction is the one that saves people weeks: a large share of the missing paperwork is the sort that nobody sends unless asked.

## Step 3: Ask about the events, not just the income

Life events change what is needed and they are invisible on a bank statement. Ask about the year:

- Moving, especially across a border
- Changes in relationship or household status
- A birth, adoption, or a dependant gained or lost
- Death of a family member, and anything inherited or administered
- Buying, selling, refinancing, or letting property
- Starting or stopping a business, or closing one down
- Study, training, or anything with fees attached
- Significant medical expenditure
- Charitable giving
- Working from home, or a change in how work is done
- Any correspondence at all from the tax authority, which people frequently do not mention and which sometimes changes what has to be filed

Each of these implies documents. List the document, not the treatment.

## Step 3b: Where an event splits the year in two

Some events divide a year into two periods that are documented separately even though they concern the same assets: a death, an emigration or arrival, a business ceasing, a marriage or separation where it affects filing. The paperwork does not divide itself, and reconstructing which side of the line something falls on afterwards is far harder than recording it at the time.

So where such an event exists, make it the organising spine of the whole list rather than a category within it. Say plainly that there are two sets of records about the same things, and tell the user to label every document with which side of the date it belongs to as they obtain it. That costs seconds during gathering and days afterwards.

Do not say what filing each period requires. Say that the two periods are documented separately, and put the question of what each one needs on the list for the preparer.

## Step 4: Flag what cannot be recovered later

The single most valuable section, because everything else on the list will still be obtainable in six months and these will not.

- **Records from institutions that close, merge, or are left behind.** A closed bank account, a former employer, a platform that shuts down, a broker changed mid-year. Get these first.
- **Anything held only in an online portal.** Access frequently ends with the relationship, and statements are often retained for a limited period. Download rather than bookmark.
- **Cost basis and acquisition records for anything sold**, which may reach back years and are the commonest catastrophic gap. When an asset is sold, what is needed is what it cost and when, and the record of that is often much older than the year being filed.
- **Receipts for anything paid in cash**, which have no electronic trace.
- **Mileage, usage, and time records**, which are reconstructible in theory and never accurately in practice, and which have to be kept contemporaneously to be worth anything.
- **Anything belonging to someone who has died**, where access closes quickly and formally. Two specific warnings belong here rather than anywhere else: notifying institutions is necessary and correct and it also closes portals, often before anyone has seen what was in them, so download first where lawful access exists; and paper in a property is sometimes the only surviving record of anything acquired decades ago, so nothing should be cleared until it has been searched.

Tell the user to get this category first and to save it somewhere outside the issuing platform.

## Step 5: Say when things arrive

A checklist with no timing produces someone filing without a document that was always going to turn up in February.

For each item, mark whether it arrives automatically and roughly when, whether it must be requested, or whether the user has to compile it themselves. Then note that some documents are reissued or corrected after first being sent, which is worth knowing before filing early on the strength of the first version.

Where a user is up against a deadline, do not state what the deadline is. Say that the date is published by the tax authority, that extensions and their conditions exist in many places, and that a preparer will know. Then prioritise the list so the hardest-to-obtain items are started immediately.

## Step 5b: When the records have a gap

Very common, and it changes what the deliverable is: the user is not gathering, they are reconstructing.

Say plainly that the gap is rebuilt from primary sources rather than from memory, and give the order, because working outward from the most complete record is what makes it tractable: the bank first, since almost everything eventually appears there; then each platform's own export, which explains what the bank lines are; then any physical or paper record; then the calendar, diary, and email, which supply the dates and occasions that nothing else captures. A calendar is frequently the single most useful reconstruction tool anyone has and nobody thinks of it as a financial record.

Do not restart an abandoned spreadsheet from recollection. The reconstruction replaces it.

**Where nothing exists for a particular date or item, record that it is missing rather than estimating it.** Marking a gap is a legitimate and honest state that a preparer can work with. A plausible number invented to fill a hole is indistinguishable, later, from a recorded one, and it is the point at which an organisation exercise turns into something else. Give the user a list of the specific gaps to hand over alongside the records.

**Where a figure could only have been captured on a date that has passed** — a count, a reading, a valuation — say that it cannot be created retrospectively, and put it to the preparer rather than filling it in.

## Step 6: Say what to do with it

- **One place, digital, backed up**, with a folder per tax year. The organising principle is the year, because that is how everything will be asked for.
- **Name files so they sort**: year, then issuer, then what it is.
- **Keep the originals** of anything that arrives on paper and is not reissuable.
- **Keep records for as long as required**, which is a period set by the jurisdiction and sometimes longer for particular items such as property. Tell the user to check the required period rather than assuming, and note that it is often longer than people expect.
- **Write down the questions** the gathering raised, as a list to put to a preparer. This is worth as much as the documents, because the questions are what would otherwise become guesses.

## Step 7: Say when this is not a checklist problem

Recommend a professional plainly, and say why, where any of these are present:

- More than one jurisdiction, in any form
- A business with employees, stock, or premises
- Property, particularly where it has been let or sold
- Trusts, estates, or acting for someone else
- Assets sold where the acquisition records are incomplete
- Cryptocurrency beyond a trivial amount, or high-volume trading
- Equity compensation of any kind
- A previous year that was filed wrongly, or not filed
- Any letter from the tax authority that is not routine

Say what the professional needs from them, which is the list this skill produced, and note that arriving organised is what makes the fee small.

## Step 8: Deliver

Give back a list they can work through, grouped so it is actionable rather than exhaustive:

1. **The assumptions** — jurisdiction, year, and anything inferred. These go at the top, as headings with what changes if each is wrong, not in a closing note. The whole list is conditional on them.
2. **Get these first** — the irrecoverable ones from step 4
3. **Will arrive on its own** — with roughly when
4. **Must be requested** — with who to ask
5. **Compile yourself** — with what it has to show
6. **Questions for a preparer or the tax authority** — the things that came up and were not answered here

Where one source needs both an automatic document and a requested one, list it under whichever action the user has to take, and note the other alongside it rather than splitting the source across two groups. The groups exist to sort actions, not to classify paperwork.

Keep it to what applies. A user handed forty items of which eight are theirs will use none of them.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Stating a rate, threshold, allowance, or deadline. All change, all are trivially checkable, and being wrong here is expensive.
- Answering whether something is deductible or taxable, rather than naming the document that evidences it.
- Producing a generic list, which is both too long and missing the items specific to this person.
- Building a list without establishing the jurisdiction and the tax year, which makes it wrong rather than rough.
- Asking about documents rather than about income sources, which finds only the paperwork the user already knew about.
- Skipping the life events, which are invisible in the paperwork and change what is needed.
- Leaving the irrecoverable items in the middle of the list instead of first.
- Omitting cost basis for assets sold, which is the gap that cannot be fixed later.
- Failing to say that a situation genuinely needs a professional.
- Asserting procedural mechanics — who issues what, what a form is for, which body handles it — with the fluency of something checked. This is the likelier failure than a wrong rate, and it reads as more authoritative.
- Hedging around a structural fact about how the system is shaped, which produces vagueness rather than safety.
- Burying a load-bearing assumption at the bottom of a list built entirely on it.
- Estimating a figure into a gap, which is where organising quietly becomes something else.
- Rebuilding a lapsed record from memory instead of from the primary sources that still exist.
- Treating the questions the gathering raised as noise rather than as part of the deliverable.
